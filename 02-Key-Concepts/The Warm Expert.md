# The Warm Expert

## What It Is

A **warm expert** is the trusted human who translates a new technology for someone else — the daughter who sets up her mom's tablet, the colleague who shows you how to get the AI to stop being weird, the friend who explains why you shouldn't paste student names into a chatbot. The term comes from research on how people actually adopt technology: almost nobody learns a new tool from a manual. They learn it from a person they trust.

In the AI era, the warm expert's job has quietly expanded. It's no longer just "here's the button to click." New research on how younger adults support older adults using AI (arXiv:2608.24297, August 2026) found warm experts now also help people **judge appropriate uses, decide what to disclose, assess whether an output is credible, and decide when AI advice is safe to act on.** The warm expert isn't just tech support anymore. They're a trust bridge.

## Why It Matters for Moving Beyond Prompting

The operator → orchestrator shift is usually told as a story about tools — better prompts, better workflows, better agents. But in the real world, **the shift spreads through people.** Someone shows you the review-first pattern. Someone sets up your first pipeline. Someone says "no, don't just paste the whole spreadsheet in" at the right moment. Those people are warm experts, and they are the hidden infrastructure of every successful adoption story in this wiki — the person at the bakery's Meetup who introduced review-first, the curriculum coordinator who built the district's first workflow, the teammate who teaches the delegation move over lunch.

The 2026 study adds a warning that matters for anyone playing this role: **help often solves the immediate problem without leaving reusable calibration knowledge.** Because AI rarely produces visible breakdowns, the supporter has limited signals for when help is needed — and the person being helped often can't tell you what they need. The result: you fix today's confusion, but next week they're confused again, because the *judgment* didn't transfer, only the answer.

Adoption that sticks isn't the adoption where someone does it for you. It's the adoption where the warm expert leaves you with a better *check* — not just a better result.

## How to Spot It in Your Day

- **You have a warm expert.** There's a person you text before you try anything new with AI. Notice what you actually ask them for: a fix, or a way to think about it?
- **You ARE a warm expert.** Someone at work calls you "the AI person." People come to you with their tool problems — and, increasingly, with "is this safe to use?" questions.
- **The tell-tale failure:** the same person asks you the same kind of question three times. The help isn't transferring. You're solving problems; you're not building calibration.
- **The invisible need:** because AI doesn't visibly break down, the people you support often don't know when to ask. The questions arrive late or not at all — which is exactly when the small confusion becomes an expensive mistake.

## Try This

**The 5-Minute Warm Expert Swap** — do both halves this week:

1. **As the learner:** pick one thing you do with AI that you learned from someone else. Ask your warm expert to explain *their check* — "how do you know when to trust the output?" — instead of a fix. Write down their answer as one sentence you can reuse.
2. **As the supporter:** next time someone asks you for help with AI, use the **consentful-help pattern** from the research: before fixing, ask "do you want me to just show you, or do you want to do it with me while I explain?" Then — this is the key — end by naming the *judgment*, not just the steps: "the thing to check here is whether the numbers match the spreadsheet, not whether it sounds confident."

That second half is the whole game: **help that leaves a check behind is help that compounds.** See also [[The Coaching Stance]] — the warm expert who teaches is a coach, and the warm expert who only fixes is a crutch.
