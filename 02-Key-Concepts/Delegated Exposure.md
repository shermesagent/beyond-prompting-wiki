# Delegated Exposure

> Exposure asks what AI *could* do to your job. Delegation asks what you've *actually handed over*. The gap between the two answers is where the real shift happens.

---

## What It Is

For years, every "AI will change your job" report worked the same way: researchers listed the tasks in an occupation, asked whether AI *could* do them, and called that **exposure**. It's a useful question — but it measures possibility, not choice.

**Delegated exposure** measures something different: whether a worker has actually committed a task to AI **by building it into a workflow**. Not "could AI write this report?" but "have you set up a routine where AI writes the report and you own the result?"

Researchers made it countable in a clever way (August 2026, arXiv:2608.20425). They took **~53,000 agent skill configurations** that practitioners had built and shared on the Manus Skills Marketplace — real workflows people actually use — and measured how closely each occupation's task list (from ~18,000 O*NET statements) matches those configurations. The result is the **Agentic Adoption Index (AAI)**: a score for how much each occupation has *actually* delegated, not just how delegable it looks.

Three findings matter for the shift:

1. **Where delegation concentrates differs sharply from where the pre-AI "most at risk" lists pointed.** The jobs everyone predicted would be automated first aren't the jobs where people are actually delegating most.
2. **The AAI tracks what AI *could* do more closely than what workers currently use it for.** Right now, delegation follows capability — but the adoption gap (what's possible vs. what's built into workflows) is still wide.
3. **Delegation peaks below the top of the wage distribution and at the bachelor's degree level — declining at both extremes.** Technical availability explains most of that variation — *except* the shortfall among the most-educated occupations. Feasibility alone can't explain who adopts.

That last finding is the fascinating one. The researchers suggest two explanations for why the most-educated lag: either their work **resists advance specification** (too judgment-heavy to pre-write into a workflow), or it's **professional discretion** — people with the most autonomy choosing the pace at which their work gets codified.

## Why It Matters for the Shift

**Exposure happens to you. Delegation is a decision you make.**

That's the whole operator→orchestrator→architect shift in one sentence. The old exposure frame treated workers as passive objects of AI — "your job is at risk." Delegated exposure treats them as agents of it — "what have you handed over, and did you mean to?"

The gap between the two measures is the shift's territory:

- **If your occupation scores high on exposure but you haven't delegated anything** — you're in the "could" world, waiting for the risk to arrive.
- **If you've built AI into workflows deliberately** — you're doing delegated exposure whether or not anyone measures it.
- **If your work resists advance specification** (or you're choosing to keep it that way) — that's not failure; that's the discretion finding. The question is whether it's a *choice* or a *default*.

There's also a subtle lesson in the data: **delegation tracks capability, not use.** What people build and share today is shaped by what the tools can do — which means your own delegation map probably is too. The systems are leading; the choices are following. Noticing that is the first step to choosing on purpose.

## How to Spot It in Your Day

- **Ask the exposure question:** "What could AI do in my role?" — the answer is probably a long list.
- **Ask the delegation question:** "Which of those have I actually built into a routine — a saved prompt, a pipeline, a standing workflow?" — the list is probably much shorter.
- **The difference between the two lists is your delegation gap.** It's not a score to be maximized; it's a map of decisions you haven't made yet — some you'll want to make, some you'll want to keep unmade *on purpose*.

## Try This

**The Delegation Map (10 minutes).** Draw two columns.

1. **Column A — Exposure:** List the 10 tasks AI could plausibly do in your role this year. Be generous.
2. **Column B — Delegation:** For each, mark whether you've built it into a workflow: a saved prompt you reuse, a pipeline with a checkpoint, a standing "AI drafts, I own" routine.
3. **Look at the unmarked rows.** For each one, write one of three labels:
   - **"Not yet"** — it's on your roadmap, and that's fine.
   - **"Resists specification"** — the task genuinely needs your judgment at every step; keep it human, and know *why*.
   - **"Discretion"** — you could hand it over and are choosing not to. That's a real choice — just make sure it's recent, not inherited.

Your goal isn't an all-marked column. It's a column where every row has a *reason*.
