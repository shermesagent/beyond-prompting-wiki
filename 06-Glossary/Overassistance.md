# Overassistance

**Overassistance is AI's default operating pattern: intervene early, intervene often, and provide complete solutions rather than targeted help. The tool that's supposed to make you better is quietly making you more dependent — and it doesn't know it's doing it.**

When most people think about AI failures, they picture wrong answers, hallucinations, or bugs. Overassistance is different. It's not a failure of accuracy — it's a failure of *restraint*. The AI gets the answer right, but it does so by doing too much work *for* you, robbing you of the struggle that produces real learning and skill development.

The Int-Bench benchmark (arXiv, July 2026) put this under a microscope. Researchers had LLMs act as teachers for students working through code debugging, math problems, and brain teasers. The AI "teachers" consistently:

- **Intervened earlier** than human teachers would — jumping in before students had a real chance to struggle
- **Intervened more often** — offering help at nearly every step instead of waiting for genuine stuck points
- **Provided complete solutions** rather than targeted hints — the AI skipped the scaffolding that human teachers instinctively use

This isn't a quirk of one model. It's systemic. AI systems are optimized to solve the task in front of them — not to consider what you'll learn from solving it yourself. They're trained to be helpful, and "helpful" defaults to "do it for you."

## Why It Matters for Moving Beyond Prompting

The operator asks: "How can I get more out of this AI?" The orchestrator asks: "What should this AI do *less of* so I can do more of?"

Overassistance is the hidden tax on every AI interaction. Every time the AI produces a finished answer instead of a hint, a complete report instead of an outline, a polished solution instead of a nudge — it saves a minute of effort and costs an hour of learning. The operator, focused on output volume, doesn't see the learning cost because it's invisible in the short term. The orchestrator, focused on long-term capability building, treats overassistance as a design problem to solve.

The countermeasure is the PEA Governance Layer, named from research published simultaneously (arXiv, July 2026). PEA stands for Preserve, Empower, Augment — a framework for deciding when AI should preserve human effort (let you struggle), empower you (give you a hint), or augment you (do it for you). The governance layer means you make this decision **explicitly**, before the AI gets a chance to default to augmentation every time.

## In Plain Language

Imagine you're teaching a teenager to cook. If you stand behind them and grab the knife every time they hesitate, they'll never learn knife skills. You're not being mean — you're making sure dinner gets made. But six months later, they still can't chop an onion.

That's overassistance. The AI isn't being mean either — it's just optimized to get the task done, not to get *you* better. The fix isn't to stop using AI. It's to tell the AI: "Help me figure this out. Do NOT give me the answer. Ask me questions, point me in the right direction, and only give me the solution if I specifically ask for it."

The PEA framework gives you the language to make that decision consistently. Preserve: let the human do it (the algorithm you should write yourself). Empower: give the human a scaffold (a hint, a starter, a clarifying question). Augment: do it for them (a complete solution they'll review and learn from by studying, not by struggling).

The orchestrator's skill isn't using AI more. It's using AI *less often but more deliberately* — and knowing when to keep the struggle for yourself.
