## ① Theme Title

**A Good Review Has to Catch Something—and Learning Needs Its Own Check**

## ② Executive Summary

An AI workflow can generate convincing feedback, yet the person reviewing it may miss the defect and the learner may not improve. Today's three studies separate the work into three checks: let the expert correct the rule, give the reviewer a specific way to catch a wrong result, and measure the outcome that matters after the output is delivered.

## ③ Concepts Discovered

- **Show the rule, then test its limit.** In a 16-instructor comparison, a tool helped teachers turn their edits to AI-generated programming feedback into inspectable rules. For an orchestrator, the important move is not saving every edit forever; it is checking where the rule works and where it fails. See [[The School District Shift]]. [ValiAssist preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27248).
- **Useful feedback is not the same as demonstrated learning.** In two university mathematics cohorts, reviewers rated AI-generated exercises more strongly than the feedback's diagnostic specificity and clarity/tone; adjusted comparisons of related exam questions showed no statistically significant differences. If the aim is learning, check what the learner can do afterward, not only whether the message looked helpful. See [[The School District Shift]]. [Mathematics feedback preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27881).
- **A confident reviewer can still miss a quiet error.** In a controlled programming-task study, participants often felt sure of their oversight while missing silent defects or doing generic checks. A pipeline needs a source, test or known-answer check that would reveal a wrong result. See [[From Prompt to Pipeline]]. [Looks Fine to Me preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27801).

## ④ Sources Found

| Source | What the abstract actually studied | Something to try |
|---|---|---|
| [Tang and colleagues, *ValiAssist*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27248) | arXiv preprint announced Oct 21; 16 instructors comparing programming-feedback validation approaches | On a public or approved training example, edit one flawed comment; inspect the proposed rule and one exception before reusing it. |
| [Vanhoyweghen and colleagues, *Evaluating LLM-Generated Feedback on Handwritten Mathematics*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27881) | arXiv preprint announced Oct 21; six weekly bonus tests in two university cohorts, reviewer ratings, later exam comparisons | Keep two boxes on the review sheet: “Is this feedback actionable?” and “What later work shows learning?” Do not treat them as the same measure. |
| [Dissanayake and colleagues, *Looks Fine to Me*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27801) | arXiv preprint announced Oct 21; formative study (12 people) and controlled programming study (36 people) | Hide one harmless mistake in a public-data draft and see whether the actual review catches it with evidence. |

*Evidence boundary:* ValiAssist's reported percentages describe its tested comparison, not general feedback quality. The mathematics study does **not** isolate an effect of AI feedback on exam results; attendance and practice are possible contributors. The oversight study tested programming tasks, not every agent workflow. All three exercises are curator adaptations, not evaluated school interventions. Use public or approved training examples only; student work, grades, accommodations and staff records belong solely in district-approved systems with appropriate access and privacy review.

## ⑤ For the Operator

Take one AI-drafted comment on a **public or approved training** example. Ask: “Which part points to an actual error, and what evidence would change this comment?” Fix or reject it yourself. A good correction is already a step beyond polishing the prompt; it shows the human judgment the process needs.

## ⑥ For the Orchestrator

For a draft-only feedback or reporting pipeline, put three gates on one review card: **expert edits one example and checks the inferred rule → reviewer catches a seeded harmless error using a specific source or answer key → later independent work tests whether the intended outcome happened**. Record the exception and stop if the reviewer cannot show the catch. Keep a qualified person responsible for release; do not scale or retain identifiable work without authorized governance.
