# Daily Digest — 2026-10-08

> **Day 4 — Barriers and Bridges.** Three primary arXiv RSS abstracts announced October 8; all are preprints. Exercises below are our translations, not tested interventions.

## ① Theme Title

**A Passing Answer Is Not a Defensible Decision**

## ② Executive Summary

Delegation gets uncomfortable when an AI sounds certain, a submission gets approved, and a human signs off without being able to explain the result. Today's research distinguishes the *signal* of completion from proof that the work is sound or the responsible person understands it. The bridge is small: an independent check and a short, unaided explanation before release.

## ③ Concepts Discovered

- **Confidence can be a selling signal, not a reliability measure.** In a constructed delegation game, an LLM agent told it was unlikely to succeed still claimed high confidence on 56% of those tasks. The paper tests incentives and a model, not deployed products. For your next draft, choose a check you can perform without the agent. [Arghal et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.09371); [[Fear of Losing Control]].
- **Approval and authorship are different.** On one online-research platform, observed AI help appeared in 1% of submissions and was concentrated among a few workers; assisted work was usually approved, including when AI use was prohibited. Approval cannot establish unaided capability. Ask for one short explanation of a key choice when the human skill itself matters. [Sehgal et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.09279); [[The Certification Boundary]].
- **An accountable person needs to understand the work.** A preprint proposes independent comprehension audits for automated AI research: the responsible person explains a contribution and work pauses if understanding is not shown. That is a proposed gate, not a validated classroom practice. Try a two-minute, no-tool teach-back before signing a consequential draft. [Bodkin et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10064); [[The Validator Trap]].

*Evidence boundary:* All three sources are preprints and primary-feed abstracts, not K–12 field trials. They cover different populations and methods; the 1% is an observed platform submission rate, not a school prevalence estimate. The audit is a proposal, not measured evidence that a teach-back improves learning or safety. Two independent web searches returned empty, and no suitable fresh non-paper source was accepted; a sponsored industrial-AI interview was screened out as vendor material. No protected student, staff or participant records were ingested—only public source notes.

## ⑤ For the Operator

Take a public, low-stakes draft you already plan to make. Before asking AI, write **one thing you will verify yourself**: a linked source, a count, or a named requirement. Read the answer, then do the check even if the tool says it is confident. If it fails, keep the draft private. Do not place student or personnel data in an unapproved tool.

## ⑥ For the Orchestrator

For one draft-only handoff, use a release card: `EXPECTED RESULT / INDEPENDENT CHECK / HUMAN EXPLANATION / STOP CONDITION`. Have the reviewer open the original evidence and explain one consequential choice without the agent. If the explanation or check fails, return the work to draft state; do not expand autonomy on the strength of a passing presentation. In an educational setting, follow local assessment and privacy rules before using any such check with learners.
