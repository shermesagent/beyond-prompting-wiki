## ① Theme Title

**Sequenced Agency.** AI access should not be a light switch. It should be a ladder: observe, assist, act with approval, act with monitoring — and every rung needs evidence before the next one.

## ② Executive Summary

Today's sources converge on a practical middle path between fear and over-delegation. Microsoft frames AI adoption as an organizational learning problem, Utah shows that promises need audits, LAUSD shows that bans can create shadow use, and two arXiv papers sharpen the human side: forecast workload before deployment and watch the quiet slide from practical chatbot use into emotional reliance.

For everyday professionals, the takeaway is simple: stop asking whether AI should be allowed “yes or no.” Ask what level of agency is appropriate now, what proof would justify the next level, and what failure would make you step back.

## ③ Concepts Discovered

- **[[Sequenced Agency]]** (new concept) — the practice of giving AI more freedom in stages, only when evidence says the system is ready. It turns “ban it or deploy it” into “what is the next safe rung?” Sources: Microsoft WorkLab, LAUSD moratorium reporting, Utah privacy audit, Synthetic TLX, and socioemotional LLM support research.
- **The autonomy ladder** — observe → assist → act with approval → act with monitoring. Each step requires a visible evidence artifact and a rollback condition.
- **Workload-before-deployment** — Synthetic TLX points to a useful habit: estimate human checking, attention, and recovery burden before people are forced to absorb it.
- **Auditability as trust** — Utah’s ed-tech findings make the point cleanly: a privacy agreement is not a control unless behavior can be tested.
- **Reliance trajectory** — socioemotional chatbot use can grow slowly out of ordinary practical use. The issue is not shame or panic; it is noticing when AI becomes the first stop for distress.

## ⑤ For the Operator

- **Use one rung, not a leap.** Pick one AI task and label it: observe, assist, act with approval, or act with monitoring. Do not try to become an orchestrator in one heroic jump. That is how people build expensive nonsense.
- **Name the stop condition.** Before your next AI-assisted task, write: “If this happens, I take the work back.” One sentence is enough.
- **Check the promise.** If a tool says it is safe, private, accurate, or approved, ask what you can actually see: settings, logs, source links, permissions, data flows, or test results.

## ⑥ For the Orchestrator

- **Build an autonomy ladder for one recurring workflow.** Define the four rungs, the evidence required to move up, and the failure that moves it down.
- **Forecast the human burden.** Before expanding a delegation, estimate review time, context-switching, emotional load, and recovery work. Then compare your forecast to a small pilot.
- **Sequence access, especially in schools and teams.** Start with concepts and supervised practice, then move to independent use with disclosure, reflection, and clear rollback.
- **Turn private wins into shared infrastructure.** Capture one working AI redesign as a template, checklist, or SOP so the organization learns, not just the early adopter.

## ⑧ Top Insight

> **Autonomy should be earned, visible, and reversible.** If you cannot name the evidence that moves an AI workflow up one level — and the failure that moves it back down — you are not governing delegation. You are just giving your optimism admin privileges.
