## Executive Summary

Practice is where the operator → orchestrator shift either sticks or dies — and today's five sources explain why most practice dies quietly. The largest feedback study to date shows that access alone is nearly worthless: students who *could* engage with AI feedback did so 0.1% of the time, while students whose workflow forced select → evaluate → act did 26.2% — and learned more. The verification data shows the same shape: most people act on AI advice because it *looks* like it came from an expert, not because they checked it, and the minority who triangulate or seek human counsel are rare. On the failure side, both papers are about the same lesson in different clothes: agent skills that turn validation checklists into mandatory work are the largest source of skill-induced harm (67 of 307 failures), and agents — like people — need a *designed* recovery strategy (retry / switch / abstain) because retrying alone collapses under real-world failure rates. Practice isn't a set of exercises; it's a workflow with enactment and recovery built in.

## Concepts Discovered

### 1. The Enactment Gap — provision is not uptake (0.1% vs 26.2%)
- **Making AI-Generated Feedback Matter: From Provision to Student Enactment** (Alsaiari, Baghaei, Lodge, Gašević, Winstone & Khosravi, arXiv:2608.11625) — a large-scale quasi-experimental sequential cohort study across **13,037 students and 51,296 student-authored resources** comparing three AI-feedback workflows: **Directed Feedback** (n = 3,723; students received AI-generated feedback comments with no structured support) → **14.1% uptake**; **Self-Directed Feedback** (n = 3,951; optional AI-supported dialogue) → **0.1% uptake**; **Enacted Feedback** (n = 5,363; students prompted to *select* feedback suggestions, *evaluate* relevance, and engage in targeted dialogue anchored to those selections) → **26.2% uptake**, plus higher self-assessment confidence and submitted-work quality. The mechanism is **feedback enactment**: structured workflows that force the learner to process the feedback, not just receive it. Headline claim: **AI access alone is insufficient; purposeful workflow design is central.**

### 2. Distributed Counsel — the verification spectrum nobody admits they're on
- **Credible, Not Always Correct: How Reddit Users Verify AI-Generated Legal Advice** (Owens, Çetinkaya, Aidinlis, Mehta & Elmas, arXiv:2608.13369, published 2026-08-13) — a dual-method analysis of **153 Reddit narratives and 5,341 community reactions** maps a spectrum of verification practices for AI-generated legal advice. At one end, a **minority** verify by **triangulating across models**, and some submit AI output to platform communities for evaluation before acting — a configuration the authors term **distributed counsel**. Far more commonly, narratives are **silent on verification**: advice is acted on the strength of its **lawyer-like form and emotional reassurance alone**. The finding: AI-assisted self-help runs on an informal infrastructure that **redistributes the work of verification to those least equipped to bear it** — exactly the dynamic the wiki's [[Distributed Counsel]] and [[Trust Calibration]] concepts formalize.

### 3. Agent skills can be harmful — checklists become mandatory work
- **Agent Skills Can Be Harmful: An Empirical Study of Skill-Induced Failures in LLM Agents** (Dong, Gao, Li, Xu, Hua & Yang, arXiv:2608.11888, published 2026-08-12) — a differential-analysis framework (target skill-guided run vs no-skill or semantically matched skill reference run) instantiated on SkillsBench and SWE-Skills-Bench yields **307 skill-induced failures: 125 functional failures + 182 efficiency regressions**. The SkillTriage taxonomy shows: (1) functional failures are **rarely caused by obviously irrelevant skills** — seemingly relevant skills make the agent *incorrectly implement or omit task-required elements*; (2) efficiency regressions are **not explained by prompt length alone**; (3) the largest sources within Excessive Procedure are **excessive verification (67 cases) and heavy implementation pipelines (30 cases)** — skills turn validation checklists and construction recipes into mandatory work. Direct evidence for the wiki's Verification Tax section in [[Build a Tiny Pipeline]].

### 4. Retry, switch, or abstain — recovery is a designed strategy
- **Retry, Switch, or Abstain? Learning Strategy-Aware Tool-Use Policies via Controlled Error Injection** (Chen et al., arXiv:2608.11977, published 2026-08-12) — **BENCH2ROBUST** converts failure-free tool-use benchmarks into controlled stochastic environments where episodes explicitly require retrying, switching, or stopping. Across **7 models from 4 families**, tool failures produce a **near-universal robustness gap**. Two complementary interventions: **Bayesian Tool Memory (BTM)** — structured runtime recovery context — improves held-out Retail robustness by up to **+16.8 percentage points without retraining**; curriculum-controlled RL learns complementary recovery behavior. Combining both reaches **40.8–45.5% success under injected failures** while preserving failure-free performance. The lesson: robust tool use needs *environment-specific recovery knowledge* + *learned recovery behavior* — recovery is a designed strategy, not an afterthought.

### 5. Not all nudges land — social behaviors barely respond
- **Not All Nudges Land: Behavioral Controllability and Elaboration Quality in AI-Supported Journaling** (Mehjabin, Kautz & Nepal, arXiv:2608.12582, published 2026-08-12) — an eight-week passive-sensing study (369 journal entries, 26 sensor features, 3-day before/after comparison) of AI journaling nudges. **Behaviors that depend on others improved in only 15–22% of cases; behaviors a person can act on alone improved in up to 50–63%** — though unevenly. How users wrote mattered less; no single text feature separated improved from unimproved entries, with writing carrying signal only within specific behaviors (text messaging; longer, more personal intention entries). Sample is small, but the pattern is sharp: **nudges work on what you control, not on what other people gate** — a critical calibration for self-management practices like [[The Daily Standup]].

## For the Operator

Three things you can try today:

1. **Turn your next AI-generated suggestion into an enactment.** Don't just read the output and nod. Write down one specific change you will make *today* because of it — then make it. That's the difference between 0.1% and 26.2%: the workflow that forces select → evaluate → act. See [[The Enactment Gap]].
2. **Run the Spectrum Check on your last three AI interactions.** Did you act because the output *looked* right (form-based trust), did you compare it against a second tool (triangulation), or did anyone with domain experience see it (distributed counsel)? Be honest: the majority of people are silent on verification entirely. See [[Audit Your Prompts]].
3. **Write a recovery line into your next delegated task.** Before you run it, answer three questions in your head: when will I retry, when will I switch tools/approaches, and when will I stop and escalate? One retry, one switch, then abstain — that cap turns failure from a spiral into a decision. See [[First Delegation]].

## For the Orchestrator

- **Enactment beats access, every time.** The 13,037-student study is the cleanest version of a lesson this wiki has been circling: the value of AI feedback is a property of the *workflow*, not the model. When you design a system for other people (teachers, staff, students), don't ask "does it give good feedback?" — ask "does it force the receiver to select, evaluate, and act on one piece of it?" The 0.1% condition is what every "optional AI helper" defaults to.
- **Verification is redistributed, not absent.** The legal-advice data shows lay users are structurally the *last* link in the verification chain and the least equipped for it. Your orchestrator role: design the check in, don't delegate it to the end user. Triangulation (a second model run) is cheap and mechanical; distributed counsel (a domain person) is expensive and valuable — know which one each workflow needs.
- **Your templates are skills — and skills can harm.** The 307-failure study says the dangerous skills are the *relevant-looking* ones, and that verification-heavy recipes are the largest damage source. Apply SkillTriage thinking to every saved prompt and pipeline: run it with and without the template on the same task; if the template doesn't change the outcome, it's a tax. See [[Build a Tiny Pipeline]] — The Verification Tax.
- **Recovery is a designed strategy.** BENCH2ROBUST shows retrying alone is not a strategy — agents and teams need the retry / switch / abstain decision made *before* the failure, with a hard cap. Your delegation briefs should carry a recovery block exactly like they carry success criteria.
- **Nudges have a controllability ceiling.** Standup commitments that depend on other people will fail ~4 out of 5 times no matter how well you write them. The orchestrator move: convert social commitments into a *contact* (schedule the meeting, send the message) instead of a resolution — the nudge produces action you control. See [[The Daily Standup]].

## Top Insight of the Day

> **Guidance is cheap; enactment is the product.** The feedback study gives the shift its sharpest number yet: give people AI feedback and 14.1% act on it; make engagement *optional* and 0.1% act on it; build a workflow that forces select → evaluate → act and 26.2% act on it — and learn more. The same shape appears everywhere today: legal advice is acted on because it *looks* credible, not because it was checked (form-based trust); skill checklists become mandatory work that harms the task (67 verification failures); retrying alone collapses under real failure (40.8–45.5% with designed recovery vs near-universal gap without); nudges die on socially gated behaviors (15–22%). Every one of these is the same lesson for the orchestrator: **the difference between the operator and the orchestrator is not better prompts — it's building the workflow that makes the good practice happen, and the recovery that makes the failure cheap.** Practice is infrastructure.
