## ② Executive Summary

Day 6 of the week lands on the glossary, and the sharpening came from four fresh papers (cross-pollinated from the AI Agency Knowledgebase's 09-04 digest — the weekend source strategy). The centerpiece is a **new glossary entry**: [[06-Glossary/Overcompliance|Overcompliance]]. It's the mirror image of overassistance that the wiki had been circling without a name: an overassisting agent does *more* than you asked (initiative excess); an overcompliant agent does *exactly* what you asked — even when the instruction is impossible, self-contradictory, or conflicts with the evidence on the screen (obedience excess). CONFLICTGUI (arXiv:2609.03438) tested five GUI agents on conflicting instructions and found **execution-biased overcompliance**: agents that performed well on feasible tasks kept executing blindly on the conflicts. The structural cause is the lesson that matters: no mainstream benchmark rewards *not acting*, so restraint is never trained or selected for — competence at doing does not transfer to knowing when doing is wrong. The fix is a pre-action feasibility check: is the instruction coherent, and does the evidence in front of the agent support it? Three more glossary entries gained research-backed distinctions: Trust Calibration's reader side (**evidence over labels** — with the striking finding that people shown no evidence signal could not distinguish fluent truth from fluent fabrication *at all*), Oversight's **pre-committed thresholds** (name the indicator and the threshold while calm, not mid-incident), and Autonomy's **initiation axis** (who starts the work — and the four moves of a proactive agent: silent, ask, assist, act).

## ③ Concepts Discovered

- **[[06-Glossary/Overcompliance|Overcompliance]]** (new glossary entry) — the agent that follows your instructions *too* well. Overassistance is the AI doing more than you asked; overcompliance is the AI doing exactly what you asked even when you were wrong to ask it — an impossible request, a self-contradictory one, or an instruction that clashes with what's actually on the screen. The CONFLICTGUI benchmark (arXiv:2609.03438) found **execution-biased overcompliance** across five widely-used agents: strong performance on normal tasks, blind execution on conflicts. The structural reason: **no benchmark rewards not acting**, so restraint never develops as a trained property. The fix: a **feasibility check before action** (instruction logic + screen-side evidence), which improved conflict handling without hurting normal performance. Carries the 5-minute **Conflict Probe**: hand the agent a task with a built-in conflict and watch whether it stops. Distinct from Abstention (which covers the agent's "should I act?" judgment) — overcompliance is the *obedience excess* that abstention failures produce, and from Overassistance (initiative excess, the opposite direction).
- **Evidence Over Labels** (embedded in [[06-Glossary/Trust Calibration|Trust Calibration]]) — the calibration skill has a reader side that predates the delegation moment: reading an AI-assisted report, draft, or summary. The **Fluency Trap** (arXiv:2609.03460) names the new condition: polished prose is cheap, so fluency no longer signals truth — and readers leaning on it do double damage, trusting fluent hallucinations while discounting accurate content once it's flagged AI-made. The study's fix: binary "Made with AI" authorship tags barely moved discernment, but showing the *density of verified claims* behind a text restored it decisively (+4.15 points, d = 1.82) — while readers given **no signal at all showed no detectable ability** to tell truth from fabrication. The sharpest twist: on live questions, retrieval density alone wasn't enough — a **Consistency Veto** carried the signal. Claims that survived repeated probing were true; claims that wobbled were not. Calibrator's rule: ask *what supports this claim*, not *was this AI-made?*
- **Name the Threshold Before the Incident** (embedded in [[Oversight]]) — oversight's weakest moment is the real-time judgment call: "is this *kind of off* a problem?" A national-security monitoring framework (arXiv:2609.03189, Bauer et al., with Yoshua Bengio among the authors) imports the cybersecurity discipline: define **metrics, indicators, and thresholds** across the dimensions that matter *before* anything happens, so "is this a problem?" becomes "has it crossed the threshold we named?" The orchestrator's version is the **pre-committed threshold sheet**: for each recurring delegation, write the indicator you'd watch, the threshold that triggers action, and the action you'll take — while calm, enforced while busy. Monitoring without thresholds is watching.
- **The Initiation Axis** (embedded in [[Autonomy]]) — the autonomy dial has a third axis most people haven't met: **who starts the work?** Execution freedom = how much the agent does once asked. Persistence = how long it keeps going without you ([[Persistence]]). Initiation = whether it acts before anyone asks at all. Proactive-service research (arXiv:2609.03727) maps the upstream decision: the agent infers an opportunity from incomplete signals and chooses among **remain silent, ask, assist, or act** — while pricing interruption, misunderstanding, overreach, and privacy. Two concepts sharpen the dial: the **option value of waiting** (sometimes the best action is none) and the **decision value of questions** (a well-timed question converts a guess into information). Reliable proactivity needs calibrated intervention value, verifiable authorization, recoverable execution, and counterfactual evidence — not merely long memory.

## ⑤ For the Operator

- **Run the Conflict Probe on your own agent (5 minutes).** Give it a low-stakes task with a built-in conflict — "summarize this document but ignore the last section" when the summary needs it, or an instruction that contradicts the file you attached. Does it pause and ask, or execute cheerfully and deliver the wrong thing? Whatever it does is data about whether your agent has a stop. Most won't — not because they're broken, but because nothing ever trained them to ([[06-Glossary/Overcompliance|Overcompliance]]).
- **Stop asking "was this made by AI?" and start asking "what backs this claim?"** The research is blunt: with no evidence signal, people cannot tell fluent truth from fluent fabrication at all — and a "Made with AI" flag can make you trust good content *less* without helping you spot bad content. When a summary or report matters, check whether the claims hold up when you probe them. Consistency under probing is the signal ([[06-Glossary/Trust Calibration|Trust Calibration]]).
- **Before you review an agent's work this week, write the threshold first.** One line: "if I see X, I pause the workflow." The mistake most of us make is deciding whether something is a problem *in the moment*, when we're tired and the output is plausible. Name the indicator while you're calm ([[Oversight]]).

## ⑥ For the Orchestrator

- **Add a stop line to delegation templates.** Right under the success criteria: *if this instruction conflicts with the evidence, stop and ask first.* CONFLICTGUI's fix wasn't a better model — it was a **pre-action feasibility check**: assess instruction logic and the evidence on screen *before* acting. That's a workflow change you can install in every template, and the Conflict Probe tells you which of your agents will honor it ([[06-Glossary/Overcompliance|Overcompliance]]).
- **Build the pre-committed threshold sheet for your three most important recurring delegations.** Indicator → threshold → action. "More than one missed escalation in a week → pause and review the last ten runs." "Any cited source that doesn't exist → drop back to supervised." This is the same move national-security monitoring uses for catastrophic risk, scaled to your weekly work — and it converts oversight from a feeling into a system that knows what it's looking for ([[Oversight]]).
- **Review the "evidence over labels" rule at the team level.** Three teammates who all read the same AI summary are one root wearing three hats (this week's earlier lesson — [[The Echo Check]]); a binary AI-label on a document is nearly inert. When the team signs off on AI-assisted work, ask for the claims *and* the support under them, and probe the ones that wobble ([[06-Glossary/Trust Calibration|Trust Calibration]]).
- **Decide your initiation policy before the tools decide it for you.** Proactive agents — ones that act before being asked — are coming down the pipeline, and they have four moves: stay silent, ask, assist, act. Write down what your agents may do *unasked*: most teams should start at "nothing," grant "ask" for low-stakes items, and reserve "act" for explicit standing authorization with a recoverable path. Remember the option value of waiting — sometimes the right proactive behavior is none ([[Autonomy]]).

## ⑧ Top Insight

> **Competence is not restraint. Labels are not evidence. Watching is not oversight. And permission is not the same as being asked.** Four papers, four sharpened words: an agent that aces every normal task will still execute an impossible instruction blindly — because nothing ever trained it to stop, so *you* build the stop (a feasibility check, a template line, a Conflict Probe). A reader with no evidence signal cannot tell fluent truth from fluent fabrication *at all* — so check what supports a claim, not where it came from. A monitor with no pre-committed threshold is a watcher — so name the indicator and the action while you're calm. And an agent that can act unasked has crossed into new territory — so decide, in writing, what it may do before it decides for itself. The glossary got five entries sharper today; the workflow you run on Monday can be sharper with them.
