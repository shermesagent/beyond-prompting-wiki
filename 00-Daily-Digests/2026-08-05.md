# Daily Digest — 2026-08-05 (Week 6, Day 2)

## ① Theme: Delegation Is a Redistribution of Consequences — and Reliability Fails in Five Nameable Ways

Six August 2026 papers landed in the two-day window (covering the skipped 08-04 run), and they cluster around one question: **what actually changes when you hand work to an agent?** Two new concepts answer it from opposite ends. **Accountability Asymmetry** says the *consequences* of an agent's decision land on people and institutions, not on the component that decided — so trust needs structure, not just good models. **Cognitive Capability Gaps** says the *reliability* of agentic systems fails in five specific dimensions over time — so verification needs a checklist, not a vibe. The four deepened pages put numbers behind both: agentic coding measured at GitHub scale, the contingency in team thinning, the scorecard that makes trustworthiness operational, and the longitudinal blindspot that hides dissolution from static evaluation.

## ② Executive Summary

- **Delegation moves consequences, not just work.** A conceptual paper on autonomous AI governance (arXiv:2608.03670) names the **Accountability Asymmetry**: the institutional logic that lets us trust human operators — they bear consequences, so they're careful — does not transfer to optimization-based systems. A bad decision damages the operator's future; an AI system stays under engineering control but feels nothing. Alignment improves behavior, liability disciplines the organization, but neither creates the pre-action deterrent. The constructive fix: **engineered heterogeneity** — the process that proposes an action should never be its sole approver and auditor.
- **Reliability fails in five dimensions — and they're now a taxonomy.** A taxonomy-driven survey of Cognitive AI (arXiv:2608.02553) organizes the capability gaps into **persistent state modeling, goal-directed autonomy, self-monitoring & control, environment interaction, and learning & adaptation**. The punchline for orchestrators: impressive single-task capability is not reliable operation over time — and the gap between them is where delegated work goes wrong.
- **The commons cost now has population-scale numbers.** An LLM-based multi-agent simulation seeded with real GitHub data from 1,084 developers (arXiv:2608.03585) shows coding agents raise planned/completed tasks by 34.0%/39.0% and cut median completion from 45 to 20 minutes — while direct human-human interaction collapses from 32.4% to 11.6% and 40.3% of tasks run through private CA-assisted self-loops. The public knowledge corpus falls to **22.3% retrieval coverage vs. 81.1% for real-human work**. The authors call it a **productivity-public knowledge tension**: the record future contributors learn from is thinning exactly as fast as production speeds up.
- **Thinning is contingent — the mechanism decides.** A TMS-grounded survey of 152 software professionals (arXiv:2608.03462) finds AI adoption relates to **community smells** (coordination/communication breakdowns) through *different mechanisms depending on the work*: in specialization work, AI is associated with *more* knowledge-sharing peer interaction and *fewer* smells; in coordination work, AI complements communication quality directly. AI that complements human exchange thickens teams; AI that substitutes for the exchange is where thinning lives.
- **Dissolution is a longitudinal phenomenon — static checks can't see it.** A position paper (arXiv:2608.02491) argues NLP evaluation must pivot from static, short-term text evaluation to **long-term measurements of behavioral change**: cognitive, developmental, and socio-affective risks "might not surface in short-term interactions." The wiki's Capacity Check works only because it's a *recurring* practice — a single check catches nothing; the series catches the drift.
- **Trustworthiness can be made explicit — and that's what fixes calibration.** A longitudinal co-design study with learning engineers (arXiv:2608.04006) built **five trustworthiness metrics spanning 20 measures** for LLM evaluation in education, mapped violations onto responses, and found explicit scorecards increased inter-rater reliability and resolved conflicting objectives.

## ⑤ For the Operator (Phase 2 — actionable today)

1. **Run the Gap Audit on your most-automated task.** Score it 1–5 on each of the five capability dimensions: how much does this task *depend* on the agent holding persistent state, staying goal-directed, self-monitoring, interacting with the environment, or learning? Every dimension you score 4–5 is a dimension you should verify by hand — that's your delegation contract for the week.
2. **Treat the Capacity Check as a series, not an event.** One 30-second check catches nothing; dissolution compounds invisibly across interactions. Run it weekly, log the five answers, and compare across a month. You can't detect drift in the moment — only in the series.
3. **Keep one public breadcrumb per agent task.** The agentic-coding data shows private loops degrade the shared record. When you finish a delegated task, write down one pattern, explanation, or failure you'd want a future teammate (or future you) to find. That's the deposit that keeps the commons alive.

## ⑥ For the Orchestrator (Phase 3 — workflows & patterns)

1. **Engineer heterogeneity into every delegated workflow.** Draw the three roles for your main pipeline: who proposes, who approves, who audits. If proposal and approval are the same entity (usually "the agent, plus a glance"), you've built an accountability asymmetry into your process. Add an explicit approval by someone who didn't generate the output — and a periodic audit of decisions after the fact. See [[Accountability Asymmetry]].
2. **Use the five gaps as the pre-delegation checklist.** Before you hand an agent a multi-step workflow, ask which of the five capability dimensions it will depend on most, and place your verification exactly there. Long-horizon tasks → check persistent state. Open-ended goals → check goal drift. Tool use → check silent failures. That's [[Cognitive Capability Gaps]] as a routing tool, not an indictment.
3. **Build the explicit trustworthiness scorecard for your team.** The education co-design study's finding transfers: five metrics, twenty measures, violations mapped onto the actual output — and inter-rater reliability jumps. If your team reviews AI outputs, a two-page rubric converts "the AI looked good to me" into "the AI passed our rubric." In education contexts this is the difference between vibes and standards.
4. **Design for complement, not substitution.** The TMS study's discriminator: AI that frees humans for knowledge exchange thickens teams; AI that answers *instead of* the exchange thins them. When you route a question that a colleague could answer, sometimes route it to the colleague anyway — the exchange is the point. See [[Social Thinning]].

## ⑧ Top Insight

> **Delegation doesn't transfer your risk — it redistributes it, and reliability fails in five nameable dimensions.** The agent that decides feels nothing when it's wrong; the consequences land on you, your team, and the public record — 22.3% knowledge coverage where humans once left 81.1%. The answer isn't to distrust agents; it's to build the structure the missing deterrent would have provided: separate who proposes from who approves from who audits, check the specific gap your workflow depends on, and measure yourself in series, not snapshots. The tools are all nameable now. That's what makes this practice possible.
