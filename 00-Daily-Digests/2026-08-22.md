Agency stops being a vibe and becomes a typology (nature × mode × locus). Autonomy stops being a slider and becomes a question of *whose* autonomy and in *which mode*. ## ② Executive Summary

Five papers, one through-line — *precision is the safety layer*:

- **Agency has eight flavors** (arXiv:2608.20041, Fourie) — *A Three-Dimensional Typology of Agency for Advanced AI Systems*: agency is not one thing. The typology: **nature** (moral vs. legal agency — they come apart), **mode** (individual vs. collective), and **locus** (human vs. non-human). Eight cells. Frontier AI systems force the questions the typology makes visible: a system can hold legal agency without moral agency, act in a collective mode without being an individual, and be a non-human locus of agency that still shapes human agency. → Agent + Autonomy.
- **Sovereignty is bounded; oversight is priced** (arXiv:2608.19216, Lim) — *Bounded Sovereignty and the Control Tax: Pricing AI Oversight When the Deployer Does Not Own the Model*: four access layers — **data, model, infrastructure, interaction** — and the deployer holds different subsets of each. What you don't hold, you pay for: the **sovereignty discount** is the portion of the **control tax** (extra effort spent monitoring, logging, gating, verifying) that substitutes for access you lack. Findings: logs, gateways, and version control are the highest-value access points; **restricting the model's scope improves safety even while it reduces usefulness**. → Oversight + **new glossary entry Bounded Sovereignty**.
- **Delegating identity shrinks it** (arXiv:2509.02910, Matz et al.) — *The Basic B\*\*\* Effect: The Use of LLM-based Agents Reduces the Distinctiveness and Diversity of People's Choices*: 110,000 real-world choices from 1,000 U.S. consumers plus a controlled study of 348 participants / 12,097 choices. Using LLM agents for **identity-defining choices** (what to watch, read, buy) **reduces interpersonal distinctiveness and intrapersonal diversity**. Generic agents homogenize *between* people; personalized agents compress *within-person* diversity harder. Sequential decisions amplify the effect — each choice narrows the next. → Delegation.
- **Understanding is assessable — or it isn't understanding** (arXiv:2608.19816, Barrett et al.) — *Understanding as an Explicit and Assessable Component of Frontier AI Safety Decisions*: safety decisions currently lean on the vague claim "we understand the system." The paper makes understanding explicit with **four assessable objects**: the **decision**, the **decision-frame**, the **safety justification**, and the **system-in-context** — each with adequacy tests (internal coherence, tethering, felicitous falsehoods, external coherence). If you can't assess it, you don't have it. → Trust Calibration.
- **The human's frontier job: set conditions, recognize novelty** (arXiv:2608.11195, Li et al.) — *Long-Horizon AI Research for Grothendieck Constant: A Case Study in Human-AI Mathematical Collaboration*: an AI research system (Tool-Use Agentic) tightened the bounds on the Grothendieck constant to a record `6π/11 ≤ K_G ≤ π/(2 log(1+√2)) − 10⁻⁴` — novel enough that domain experts judged the result genuinely new. The collaboration structure: humans set conditions and verify; the AI does the long-horizon reasoning; humans recognize the novelty when it arrives. → Human in the Loop.

## ③ Concepts Discovered

- **[[06-Glossary/Bounded Sovereignty|Bounded Sovereignty]]** (new glossary entry) — the fact that you only control part of the AI stack you depend on, and that boundary determines what oversight is actually possible. Four-layer access typology (data / model / infrastructure / interaction); the **sovereignty discount** (the control-tax portion spent substituting for missing access); the rule: *state your access assumptions before you promise oversight*.
- **The Eight Flavors of Agency** — nature (moral vs. legal — they come apart), mode (individual vs. collective), locus (human vs. non-human). The typology's payoff for practice: "does the system have agency?" is the wrong question; the right one is *which cell are you dealing with, and what does that cell require of you?*
- **The Identity Dimension of delegation** — delegation is not only a capacity transaction; it's an identity transaction. Delegating identity-defining choices measurably reduces distinctiveness (interpersonal) and diversity (intrapersonal). Personalized agents compress within-person diversity harder; generic agents homogenize between people. The orchestrator rule that follows: *delegation is for execution, not for taste*.
- **The Understanding Floor** — four objects you must be able to write down before consequential delegation: the decision, the decision-frame, the safety justification, the system-in-context. Each has adequacy tests. Understanding that can't be assessed is faith, not understanding.
- **Conditions, not checkpoints** — the frontier human-in-the-loop: humans set conditions (problem framing, verification criteria, stopping rules), the AI runs the long horizon, and the human's rarest contribution is *recognizing novelty* — judging when a result is genuinely new. Demonstrated on the Grothendieck constant.

## ⑤ For the Operator

1. **Ask the eight-flavor question.** Next time you delegate, name the cell: is this a moral or a legal agency matter? Individual or collective? Human or non-human locus? You don't need the full typology — you need the habit of noticing that "agency" isn't one dial.
2. **Write your access list.** Before you promise anyone you'll "keep an eye on" an AI workflow, write down which of the four layers you actually touch: data, model, infrastructure, interaction. If the list has gaps, say so — and name what you do instead (logs, gates, reviews). That gap is your sovereignty discount.
3. **Don't delegate taste.** For identity-defining choices — what you read, watch, buy, write in your voice — the research says the agent homogenizes you, and personalization makes the within-you compression worse. Keep the taste decisions on your side of the line.
4. **Write down the four objects.** Before a consequential delegation, write one sentence for each: the decision, the decision-frame, the safety justification, the system-in-context. If you can't, your trust is faith — and that's a signal, not a sin.
5. **Know your loop role at the frontier.** When the task is long-horizon and you can't watch every step, your job narrows to two things: setting conditions (frame, criteria, stop rules) and recognizing novelty in the output. Practice both before you need them.

## ⑥ For the Orchestrator

1. **Put agency cells in the delegation contract.** The typology gives you a vocabulary for governance: specify the mode (individual vs. collective agent work), the locus (human vs. non-human decision point), and the nature of the authority transferred (legal agency ≠ moral agency — a system can be authorized to act without being authorized to judge). Contracts that can't name the cell can't be audited.
2. **Price your oversight by access, not by intent.** You can only oversee what you can reach. Audit your stack against the four layers; for each layer you don't own, budget the control tax explicitly — logging, gateways, version control are the highest-value substitutions. And remember the counterintuitive lever: **restricting scope improves safety even when it costs usefulness** — the cheapest oversight is the access you never grant.
3. **Protect taste as an organizational asset.** If your team's output *is* its distinctiveness (curriculum, brand, analysis voice), delegating identity-defining choices to agents will measurably homogenize the work — generic agents flatten between-people differences, personalized ones flatten within-person diversity. Route execution to agents; keep taste in human lanes.
4. **Require the four objects in safety reviews.** Any consequential deployment should carry written answers to: what decision does this automate, what frame is it using, why is it safe, and in what context does that hold? The adequacy tests (internal coherence, tethering, felicitous falsehoods, external coherence) are a review rubric, not a philosophy seminar.
5. **Design the frontier loop as conditions + verification.** For long-horizon agent runs, the human role is not approval-clicking. Build the loop with: explicit condition-setting up front, a verification protocol at the end, and — rarest of all — a lane for human novelty recognition. The Grothendieck case shows the division of labor that actually works.
