# Give the Handoff a Stop Point

*Monday, November 16, 2026 · Day 1: The Shift*

## Executive Summary

Moving beyond prompting is not simply letting AI take more steps. Three recent preprints suggest a better handoff: say when an old rule may be reused, show what the system actually did, and give a named person a chance to stop it when the evidence or context changes. The research is early and context-specific; the exercises below are small, safe ways to test the idea, not validated deployment instructions.

## Concepts Discovered

- **Oversight must be usable.** In a 32-person participatory workshop about implementing European AI rules, participants surfaced gaps between a written obligation, the measurement meant to show it works, and oversight as actually practiced. For our shift, naming a reviewer is not enough: say what they can inspect and where they can intervene. [Torkamaan and colleagues, preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18439). See [[What Is Beyond Prompting]] and [[The Evidence Ticket]].
- **One memory preview is not the whole permission rule.** A formal study of fictional AI memory rules found that an allowed/blocked example pair often could not identify the rule behind it. In a small pilot, some people mispredicted how their chosen rule would apply; a better preview has not yet been tested. Before saving a standing preference, try a changed situation and ask whether reuse should still be allowed. [Tonni and Shahid, preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17874). See [[The Preference Check]] and [[The Control Surface]].
- **A clean-looking ticket can hide a wrong order.** In an in-browser pizza-order demo, a small model sometimes produced well-formed tickets that did not match the typed request. A separate benchmark matcher lets visitors spot omissions; the model's reported exact-match rate on 1,357 test orders was 0.882. This is not evidence that a checker can verify arbitrary business workflows. For your own low-risk draft, compare the original request with the finished output rather than trusting its neat format. [Ahmed and colleagues, preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18284). See [[The Orchestrator Mindset]] and [[The Evidence Ticket]].

## For the Operator

Take one public or fictional drafting task. Before the prompt, write `GOAL / DO NOT CHANGE / WHAT I WILL CHECK / I APPROVE`. Inspect the original source yourself. If a tool suggests remembering your choice, test a changed example and leave memory off until you understand the boundary. Do not upload student or personnel data to an unapproved AI service.

## For the Orchestrator

For a draft-only workflow, design three checkpoints: **permission** (what can be reused and when to ask), **evidence** (request versus produced output), and **authority** (named reviewer, stop condition, and who may approve release). Intentionally omit one item from a harmless fictional order; make sure the human comparison catches it. If evidence is missing or ambiguous, pause rather than treating a neatly formatted output as proof of correctness.

## Top Insight of the Day

> Delegation is only a handoff when someone can see what happened, know what was allowed, and stop the next step.
