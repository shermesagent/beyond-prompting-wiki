## ① Theme Title

**Real-world evidence — the loop holds, and reality adds three new failure modes.** Week 8's Day 3 rotation grounds the delegation loop in deployment: AI that substitutes instead of scaffolds erodes the very capacities it's supposed to serve; long-running pipelines need mid-run oversight, not end-of-run review; readiness is a property of the challenge pairing, not of you; safety designs in care settings can silently cage the people they protect; and crossing the tool-to-entity threshold as a sender doesn't make you willing to be a receiver.

## ② Executive Summary

Five papers, one test — *does the delegation loop survive contact with reality?* Mostly yes, with amendments:

- **Substitution is the silent failure mode** (arXiv:2608.17451) — *From Substitution to Scaffolding*: AI in education is racing ahead of its consequences. The paper organizes the risk into four dimensions — cognition, agency, emotional well-being, ethics — linked by a **self-reinforcing harm cycle**: cognitive offloading reduces effort, weakens agency, compounds emotional and ethical harm. Grounding evidence: 49 IB argumentative essays, **80% reporting AI reliance reduces thinking**. Students want what learning science already prescribes: systems that withhold immediate answers, prompt recall, and encourage reflection. One design principle: **scaffold, do not substitute.** → **new concept page Scaffold, Don't Substitute** + The School District Shift.
- **Oversight moves from the end to the middle** (arXiv:2608.17834) — *AdaLens*: long-running agentic analysis fails when you review only at the end. The paper names the two requirements: **observability** (understand reasoning and evidence during the run) and **steerability** (redirect mid-run). Their system's storyline view unifies plan, execution progress, findings, and data-column provenance. Mid-run look-ins at named checkpoints: the boundary check, moved from the end to the pauses. → From Prompt to Pipeline.
- **Readiness is challenge-specific, not a trait** (arXiv:2608.17520) — *Ready for What?*: 982 participants, 15,200 evaluations of 17 AI/robotics challenges. Within the same person, a challenge rated one point more complex than usual scores ~0.21 points *lower* on readiness (p < 0.001); people who generally rate things complex show no systematic readiness deficit (p = 0.29). Significance helps; unusual complexity weakens that alignment. When a community isn't ready, the constraint is one of four kinds: **implementation, capability, assurance, resources** — name it, fix it. → Small Business Automation.
- **Protection without a release condition is a cage** (arXiv:2608.17175) — *Balancing Safety and Autonomy*: 45 cognitively impaired older adults + caregivers; five accessibility-oriented mechanisms (capability constraint, human oversight embedding, cognitive engagement maintenance, relationship regulation, risk transparency). The same mechanisms that support severe impairment **restrict mild impairment** — and the trade-off becomes *less visible* as impairment grows. Dynamic authority: state the trigger, the override path, the handback. → Fear of Losing Control.
- **The two-sided threshold** (arXiv:2608.18058) — *Delegation Asymmetry*: N = 2,894 / N = 2,617 on a major dating platform. Willingness to **send** agent-mediated communication and willingness to **receive** it are distinct constructs (ρ = 0.92, ΔBIC = 52) at different thresholds (−0.38 to deploy; +0.32 to engage; +1.39 full engagement). Deployment propensity runs ~3× engagement propensity; only **4–13% of random dyads** align. Reciprocity requirements halve interaction; receptivity-aware routing triples per-contact engagement (AUC 0.88). → The Tool-to-Entity Threshold.

## ③ Concepts Discovered

- **[[02-Key-Concepts/Scaffold, Don't Substitute|Scaffold, Don't Substitute]]** (new concept, README row 34) — the single design principle for any system that mediates thinking: **hold the thinking up, don't replace it.** Delegation that makes a task easier without building capability is substitution wearing a productivity costume — and the harm compounds through a self-reinforcing cycle (cognitive offloading → weakened agency → emotional/ethical harm).
- **Observability + steerability as habits, not features** — the review boundary doesn't have to be the end of the run; named mid-run checkpoints with running storylines give you the AdaLens move with no new interface.
- **Readiness = challenge × constraint type** — "am I ready for AI?" is the wrong question; "ready for which AI, doing what, and which constraint (implementation/capability/assurance/resources) is binding?" is the diagnostic.
- **Dynamic authority in care (and classrooms, and teams)** — the same protection mechanisms that support severe impairment restrict mild impairment; the release condition is what makes protection not a cage.
- **Delegation asymmetry** — crossing the entity threshold as a sender says almost nothing about willingness to be a receiver; consent in shared spaces is two-sided and both sides need design.

## ⑤ For the Operator

1. **Check for substitution, not just completion.** Before you hand off a task, ask: *am I building the capability or bypassing it?* If the agent's output leaves you no better at the underlying skill next time, you're substituting — schedule a scaffolded round (agent drafts, you structure, you explain back).
2. **Move one boundary check into the middle.** For any run longer than a sitting, define 1–2 named checkpoints: *"pause after the data pull; show me what you found and what you're planning next."* That's steerability as a habit.
3. **Ask "ready for what?" instead of "am I ready?"** When a task feels too big, name the constraint: implementation (no template), capability (no skill), assurance (no way to verify), resources (no time/budget). The named constraint is the fix list.
4. **Set a release condition on every protective habit.** If you're restricting an agent's autonomy (or your own), write the condition that lifts the restriction — the handback condition from Day 2, applied to yourself.
5. **Assume the other side hasn't consented.** Before you let an agent act in a shared space, ask the Day 1 question with Day 3's data: sending is easy, receiving isn't. Reciprocity is the default courtesy.

## ⑥ For the Orchestrator

1. **Design against the harm cycle.** The substitution loop is self-reinforcing: offloading → weaker agency → more offloading. Audit your workflows for tasks that should *stay* human — the preservation principle with the mechanism named.
2. **Give every long-running pipeline a storyline.** Require agents to maintain plan / progress / findings / evidence-used, and build a checkpoint into the workflow at natural pauses. End-of-run review is the weak control; mid-run steering is the strong one.
3. **Do readiness as a matrix, not an average.** Survey your team's readiness per challenge, not per person: within-person complexity effects (~0.21 points per complexity point, p < 0.001) hide exactly the barriers averaging erases. Ask *which challenges* are hard, and which of the four constraint types binds.
4. **Match protection to capacity, dynamically.** For any system you deploy for others (students, staff, clients): define the trigger that adds oversight, and the trigger that removes it. The same design that supports the least capable silently cages the most capable.
5. **Design the receiving end.** Deploying an agent into a shared space is the sender's move; the receiver's threshold is three times stricter. Build opt-in, disclosure, and a reciprocity rule into agent presence — and treat "the other party's agent contacted me" as a consent event, not a convenience.

## ⑧ Top Insight

> **Reality amends the loop: review can't stay at the end, readiness can't be averaged, and substitution is the failure mode nobody audits for. The strongest thread across all five papers is the same design move — dynamic authority. Scaffold instead of substitute, steer mid-run instead of judging at the end, match protection to capacity with an explicit release condition, and design the receiving end of delegation as carefully as the sending end. The loop holds — but only if the boundary checks move to where the risk actually lives.**
