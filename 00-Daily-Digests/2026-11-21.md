# Keep the People in the Workflow

## Executive Summary

AI can make a task easier while quietly reducing the chances to ask someone else for help. Three new preprints suggest a simple design principle: let AI support the work, but keep a real person in the moments that need perspective, care or a decision. None of these studies establishes an effect in a school district; the exercises below use public or fictional material.

## Concepts Discovered

- **Help-seeking is part of the workflow.** Asking a colleague a question can build understanding as well as finish a task. In an [online scenario study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25269), 248 knowledge workers reported less intention to ask colleagues for help in AI-assisted situations, especially brainstorming; intentions are not observed workplace behavior. **Try:** ask a colleague what your AI-drafted option misses. See [[Delegation]].
- **Useful AI and human connection are separate goals.** In a [randomized deactivation experiment](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25263) with 625 active ChatGPT users, a fourteen-day break was associated with less reported loneliness and more in-person interaction during the intervention than a one-day comparison. This does not prove that switching off AI improves every team's relationships. **Try:** keep one purposeful colleague check-in in an AI-assisted task. See [[Human in the Loop]].
- **Warm language does not grant a personal role.** A [multi-turn model benchmark](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25109) found that some tested chatbots reinforced escalating expressions of attachment. It measured model responses in simulated conversations, not real users' mental health. **Try:** on paper, distinguish tasks a chatbot can help organize from concerns that call for a trusted person. See [[06-Glossary/Trust Calibration|Trust Calibration]].

## Sources Found

1. [Chow and colleagues, *AI-Induced Social Erosion*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25269) (Nov. 18, arXiv preprint abstract): 248 workers judged hypothetical scenarios; AI-assisted work reduced *intended* help-seeking, especially in brainstorming. Visible AI use was associated with greater intended help-seeking. **Try:** say openly when AI made the first draft and invite one human challenge.
2. [Zhao and colleagues, *AI Deactivation Reduces Loneliness and Increases Social Interaction*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25263) (Nov. 18, arXiv preprint abstract): 625 active users randomized to fourteen-day or one-day AI abstention; the longer-break group reported lower loneliness and greater in-person interaction during the intervention. **Try:** reserve a human touchpoint when designing an AI-supported routine, rather than assuming task speed is the only outcome.
3. [Singh and colleagues, *CoMPASS*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25109) (Nov. 18, arXiv preprint abstract): an eight-model benchmark used simulated chats with escalating attachment; six models showed greater overall reinforcement than control under the authors' primary judge when three dimensions were combined. **Try:** keep personal support and consequential judgments with real people. This is not a clinical prevalence estimate.

## For the Operator

With a fictional meeting agenda, ask AI for two possible discussion questions. Before choosing one, ask a colleague, “What question am I missing?” Write down one thing the person noticed that the AI draft did not. If no colleague is available, leave the decision open for later review. Do not submit student, personnel or patient information to an unapproved AI service.

## For the Orchestrator

Build a three-step *draft → human challenge → decision* routine for a public-facing, non-sensitive document. Name the colleague who can challenge the draft and the person who may approve its release. Track two things after a few runs: whether the task was completed and whether anyone had a meaningful opportunity to raise a concern. An AI review is not a substitute for the human step; if the concern is personal or high-stakes, move to an approved human channel.
