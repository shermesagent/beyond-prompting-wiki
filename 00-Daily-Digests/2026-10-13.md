# Daily Digest — 2026-10-13

> **Day 2 — Key Concepts.** Three October 9 arXiv preprints read from their primary abstracts and publication metadata. One is theoretical, one is a desktop-assistant field study, and one tests computer-use agents on benchmarks. No private records were ingested.

## ① Theme Title

**Watch the Work. Ask About the Reason.**

## ② Executive Summary

Agents can learn from the traces people leave, but a trace is not a full explanation of what someone meant. Today's sources separate three things that are easy to collapse: an observed choice, a person's stated preference, and a reusable rule for a future task. Better delegation asks for the missing reason before it saves a default.

## ③ Concepts Discovered

- **[[The Preference Check]] — an action is a clue, not a standing instruction.** A [theoretical paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13185) shows that, under its model, the same actions can reflect different underlying preferences. Before an AI saves a rule about someone, ask if their action reflects a choice or a workaround. It matters when a one-off prompt becomes a workflow default; this is a proposed caution, not a tested intervention.
- **Show the step; explain the exception.** [Work2Skill](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12984) uses 350 human desktop demonstrations to build reusable AI procedures with step dependencies and completion checks; it reports better results than no-skill baselines on two benchmarks. Ask a colleague what would make them change one step, and add that stop condition to [[The Rule Capture Problem]]. Do not record sensitive screens to try this.
- **A friendly exchange need not change permissions.** In a [desktop-assistant study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13156), people who renamed an assistant tended to interact differently and prefer certain kinds of replies. Ask whether they want a warmer tone *for this task*; do not infer consent for more access or action. See [[Co-Construction Blindness]]. This is an association in one setting, not proof that naming causes a preference.

*Evidence boundary:* The preference result is a mathematical model, the naming study is observational, and the demonstration result is measured on computer-use benchmarks. None establishes that a new district workflow improves learning or that private activity should be mined for preferences.

## ⑤ For the Operator

Pick a public, non-sensitive recurring task. Before asking AI to repeat yesterday's approach, write one sentence: “I did it that way because ___; next time, change course if ___.” If another person will use the result, ask whether your assumption also fits their needs. Do not paste identifiable student or staff activity into an unapproved tool.

## ⑥ For the Orchestrator

For a **draft-only** procedure, use a small review card: `Observed step / Human reason / Exception / Completion check / Who confirms the default / How to change it`. Run it on a public sample, then have the responsible person approve the rule before reuse. Keep communication style and access permissions separate; neither a friendly opener nor a recorded click grants new authority. See [[Intent Scaffolding]] and [[The Preference Check]].

## ⑧ Top Insight of the Day

> **A click tells you what happened. A person can tell you why. Don't automate the guess between them.**
