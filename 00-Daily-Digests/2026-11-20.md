# Check the Finish Line Before You Hand Off

## Executive Summary

Delegating well means saying what a finished task must satisfy, checking the actual result, and deciding which actions still need a person. Today's three preprints offer a practical sequence for a small, safe workflow: define a check, reuse it carefully, and keep permission to act separate from permission to draft.

## Concepts Discovered

- **A finish line you can inspect.** List a must-have, an independent evidence check, and a reader-quality sign before asking AI to work. This matters because a smooth response can still miss the task; the [EverydayBench preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25146) evaluates real-world digital tasks with explicit constraints, live-source checks and deliverable quality. Try it with a fictional event agenda.
- **A check that travels only when it fits.** Keep a useful review criterion from one task, but ask what changes in the next. This helps the operator become an orchestrator without turning old mistakes into universal rules. [RubricDuo](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25903) studies how to adapt earlier evaluation criteria to new questions; the proposed exercise below was not tested in that study.
- **Permission is part of the task.** Specify whether AI may inspect, draft, or submit, and who can stop it. A [laboratory-agent demonstration](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25043) constrained and audited changes to an experimental workflow; it does **not** validate autonomous action in a school or office. See [[The Line You Draw]].

## Sources Found

1. [Huang and colleagues, *EverydayBench*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25146) (Nov. 18, arXiv preprint abstract): 77 human-authored everyday digital tasks across eight domains; assessment includes constraints, cited-page claims, and final quality. **Try:** define three distinct checks for one public or fictional deliverable. Not a measured workplace success rate.
2. [Deng, Huang and Zhao, *From Cases to Criteria*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25903) (Nov. 19, arXiv preprint abstract): RubricDuo adapts earlier cases and criteria for task-specific evaluation; authors report gains against tested comparison methods. **Try:** reuse one check only after naming how the new task differs. Human review remains essential for consequential judgments.
3. [Liu and colleagues, *Closing the Analyze-to-Design Gap*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25043) (Nov. 18, arXiv preprint abstract): a lab agent operated within human-defined limits with authorized, auditable state changes. **Try:** label one AI step `draft only` and reserve `send` or `change records` for explicit approval. This is a lab demonstration, not a district deployment.

Source captures are abstract-based, saved in `raw/articles/`, and registered in `sources/sources.jsonl`; full papers were not independently reviewed.

## For the Operator

Take five minutes with a fictional meeting agenda. Write `MUST INCLUDE: time and location`, `CHECK AGAINST: the sample invitation`, and `READER READY WHEN: someone can tell what to do next`. Ask AI to draft; check the finished agenda yourself. Note one error your three checks did not catch in [[The Reliance Audit]]. Use public or fictional information only—no identifiable student or personnel details in an unapproved AI tool.

## For the Orchestrator

Make a two-step practice workflow: AI drafts an agenda, then a person checks a [[The Evidence Ticket|finish-line ticket]] before anyone sends it. Save a review criterion that caught a real error, but adapt it to the next document instead of applying it blindly. Set `inspect and draft: allowed; send: human approval required; stop owner: named person`. If evidence is missing, mark the claim unverified and do not release it.

## Top Insight of the Day

**A better instruction asks for a draft; a better delegation says what counts as done, how you'll check it, and what the AI is not allowed to do.**
