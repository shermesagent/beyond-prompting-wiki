## ① Theme Title

**The Check Is the Job.** Last week's question was "what would have to happen for this answer to be wrong?" — this week's research answers it in five parts, and none of them are comfortable. The rules you write can't hold the judgment the work actually requires (rules capture know-what, not know-how). The reviewer you trust may share the author's blind spots (same family, same blind spots). Your evaluation of the machine is polluted by its marketing before you even start (expectations beat performance in moving your opinion, β = 0.47 vs. −0.01). The delegated artifact can fail silently and look finished (all 30 injected failures in vibe-coded apps passed visual inspection). And the human checkpoint you're relying on can be a formality — the clinic audit found a verified failure in one note in three, signed by clinicians. None of this means delegation is broken. It means delegation is a craft: the person who builds checks that actually check is the person who can safely delegate. That's the orchestrator.

## ② Executive Summary

- **Your written rule is a lossy copy of the job.** AI enters organizations through prompts, playbooks, and SOPs — but organizations operate on the procedure *plus* negative boundaries, runtime judgment, and learning history that never make it into the text. The paper's scaffold (O-I-B-A-R) shows how to externalize the missing boundaries — and its key design object, the *suspension*, says what to do when a dimension matters but its value is unresolved: measure, ask, retrieve, or escalate to a human. (arXiv:2608.29055)
- **The reviewer inherits the author's blind spots.** In the systems examined, the agent reviewing the work came from the same model family as the agent that produced it. CrossAudit's fix: different vendor, human-written rulebook, audit history as a git record, and "a model blocks only by citing a rule." Two vendors read the same rulebook differently — the difference is the signal. (arXiv:2608.28631)
- **You're rating the pitch, not the product.** N=162 users on the *same* models, told different stories about them: impressions after use tracked expectations (β = 0.47) and confidence (β = 0.47), not performance (β = −0.01, n.s.). The label even changed interaction style — oversold users micromanaged with directive prompts; undersold users collaborated. (arXiv:2607.05113)
- **Delegated work can fail silently and look done.** Vibe-coded apps routinely contain silent behavioral failures — the interface looks right, the data flow is wrong. Stating intent in interface terms and checking deterministically caught all 30 injected violations with zero false positives; prompting frontier models to find the same bugs failed. (arXiv:2608.28880)
- **"A clinician signs every note" was a formality, not a filter.** A verified census of three AI scribes found one note in three (31.3%) carried a verified failure — and the audit instrument moved the finding more than the scribes did (the review instruction alone swung verified failures from 9.3% to 79.0%). (arXiv:2608.31017)

## ③ Concepts Discovered

- **[[The Rule Capture Problem]]** (new concept page) — the written rule (prompt, SOP, playbook) is a lossy copy of the job: it captures know-what, not know-how. The AI receives the procedure; the organization runs on the procedure plus negative boundaries, runtime judgments, and learning history. The fix isn't a longer prompt — it's externalizing the boundaries with the O-I-B-A-R scaffold (OPEN / IS / BUT / ACTION / RESULT), where the BUT clause is the gold (a concrete failure contains more information than "not this") and the *suspension* is where human-AI handoffs are born (when a dimension matters but its value is unresolved: measure, ask, retrieve, escalate). This is the research version of why "write a better prompt" has a ceiling.
- **The Independent Reviewer** — the independence requirement made mechanical: the reviewer must come from a different vendor than the producer, against a human-written, versioned rulebook, with the audit history kept as a readable record and "a model blocks only by citing a rule." Same-family review is re-reading, not checking. (Embedded in [[The Review-First Pattern]].)
- **The Instrument Effect** — a failure rate depends on the instrument as much as on the thing being audited: same evidence, review instruction alone swings verified failures from 9.3% to 79.0%; reviewer family roughly doubles the flagged-note share. Before you believe any accuracy number, ask what instrument produced it. (Embedded in [[The Review-First Pattern]] and [[From Prompt to Pipeline]].)
- **The Expectation Effect** — your evaluation of an AI tracks what you were told about it, not what it did: expectations β = 0.47 vs. performance β = −0.01; the label even changes your interaction style (oversold → micromanaging directive prompts, undersold → collaborative briefs). "This model is great" is often "this model met my expectations." (Embedded in [[Trust Calibration]].)
- **The Silent-Failure Check** — delegated generation looks done while being wrong in the places you didn't specify; the antidote is stating the few user-visible flows that must hold and checking them deterministically — intent + deterministic check beats "another model will catch it." (Embedded in [[The Vibe Compiler]].)

## ⑤ For the Operator

- **Run the Boundary Audit today.** Take the prompt you use most — the one that works "most of the time." Write down three things a competent human knows that the rule doesn't say. For each, recall a success and a failure: what one variable changed? Write one BUT clause and one suspension line ("when this is unresolved, ask / escalate"). Add both lines to the brief next time. You've just done the orchestrator's core move without any new tooling ([[The Rule Capture Problem]]).
- **Ask the vendor question on your next review.** Is the thing checking this output from the same family as the thing that made it? If yes — same app, same model, same vibe — you have a confirmation loop, not a review loop. Even a checklist on paper beats same-vendor self-review.
- **Check your expectations before you check the output.** Before you judge a model's answer, ask yourself: what did I expect, and where did that expectation come from? The research says your impression tracks the pitch, not the product — so the calibration habit is measuring the product directly: did the output meet the standard, or just meet my story about the tool ([[Trust Calibration]])?

## ⑥ For the Orchestrator

- **Add the suspension to your delegation briefs.** For each multi-step delegation, name the dimensions that matter and mark the unresolved ones: what must be measured, asked, retrieved, or escalated when a value is unknown. A rule that guesses at an unknown is a rule that hides the handoff — the suspension is where human-AI handoffs are supposed to live ([[The Rule Capture Problem]]).
- **Engineer reviewer independence into your pipeline.** If you run anything on autopilot: different vendor for the reviewer than the producer, a human-written standard neither side edits, verdicts stored where you can re-read them, and a rule for when a model may block (only by citing the rule). The difference between two independent reviewers is the signal — not whichever one you like better ([[The Review-First Pattern]]).
- **State the flows that must hold, and check them deterministically.** For anything generated — a report, an app, a pipeline — write down the few user-visible outcomes that must be true, in the language of the interface, and check those specifically. "Another model will catch it" is a weak verification strategy: intent + deterministic check beat a smarter model's eyeball ([[The Vibe Compiler]]).
- **Demand instruments for numbers.** Every time a vendor or a colleague quotes an accuracy rate, ask: what instrument produced that number, who refuted it, and under what instructions? A failure rate is a property of the audit as much as the system — and your checkpoint is only real if it would fail without reading ([[From Prompt to Pipeline]]).

## ⑧ Top Insight

**The orchestrator's job is not delegation — it's building checks that actually check.** This week's five papers are five ways the check fails: the rule you wrote can't hold the job (know-what, not know-how); the reviewer you trust shares the author's blind spots (same family, same gaps); your evaluation tracks the marketing, not the machine (β = 0.47 expectations vs. −0.01 performance); the artifact looks done while being wrong underneath (silent failures); and the human checkpoint can be a signature instead of a check (one note in three). The through-line is not "AI is untrustworthy." It's that verification is a *craft* — independence, instruments, suspensions, and standards — and the person who can build a real check is the person who can safely delegate anything. That's the shift from operator to orchestrator in one sentence: the operator asks "what should I type?" — the orchestrator asks "what would actually catch this being wrong, and have I built it?"
