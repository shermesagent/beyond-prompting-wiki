# ① Theme: The Practice of Standing Apart

The shift from operator to orchestrator isn't learned by doing more — it's learned by stepping back. Four papers converge on a single practice-level insight: **the orchestrator's core skill is distance** — the ability to step away from AI output and evaluate it, challenge it, and own it. And distance, like any skill, takes deliberate practice.

---

# ② Executive Summary

Today's research shows that the distance skill has four components, each with its own practice requirement. First, a randomized N=220 study finds that **evaluating AI output and solving problems yourself are different muscles** — high evaluation scores don't translate to deeper understanding unless you deliberately scaffold the transfer. Second, a framework of **five capacities** (end-setting, reason-giving, contestability, refusal/revision, participation) defines what erodes when you delegate output without maintaining ownership — calling this "capacity dissolution." Third, a philosophical analysis of human-LLM interaction identifies three **structural asymmetries** — the AI bears no commitments, no accountability, no standing — and unless you practice recognizing this, you'll treat the AI like a teammate when it's a tool. Fourth, an intervention study shows that **structured reflection exercises** (breaks, drawing, small-step decomposition) measurably improve metacognitive regulation during cognitively loaded work. Together, these papers argue that the practice section of this wiki isn't optional — it's the only thing standing between productive delegation and skill erosion.

# ③ Concepts Discovered

## Concept 1: Practice ≠ Performance — The Evaluation Transfer Gap

**What it is:** A randomized A/B crossover study (N=220) in a junior-level algorithms course had students either solve algorithmic problems directly OR evaluate often-flawed GenAI-generated solutions, with roles reversed mid-semester. Students earned **significantly higher homework scores** when evaluating AI solutions — but this advantage **did not translate into better exam scores**, final grades, or problems structurally aligned with the homework.

**Why it matters:** This is the first controlled evidence that the evaluation muscle and the solving muscle are not the same. You can get all the right homework answers by evaluating AI output — and learn nothing that transfers to a similar problem without AI available. The orchestrator who only checks AI output for correctness is exercising a narrow skill. The orchestrator who can also *solve the problem themselves* is developing a durable capability. The gap between these two is where most "practice" advice falls short.

**Source:** Dickey, Mertzanidis, Psomas — "Is Solving Better Than Evaluating GenAI Solutions?" (arXiv:2607.27586, July 2026)

## Concept 2: Capacity Dissolution — The Five Things You Lose

**What it is:** When AI systems produce essays, code, reports, and decisions through which institutions recognize competence, a structural problem emerges: each technical improvement weakens the case for preserving human capacity. Kai Yao (AAAI/ACM AIES 2026) identifies five capacities that erode when output delegation becomes the default:
1. **End-setting** — the ability to define what purposes are worth pursuing
2. **Reason-giving** — the ability to explain and justify choices
3. **Contestability** — the ability to challenge, dispute, and interrogate outputs
4. **Refusal/revision** — the ability to say "no" or "not good enough" and demand better
5. **Participation** — the ability to engage meaningfully in the practices AI mediates

When a polished artifact no longer reliably evidences understanding, institutions must assess the learner's accountable relationship to AI-mediated work — not the artifact alone.

**Why it matters:** Every practice exercise in this wiki — the audit, the delegation template, the pipeline — exists to preserve one or more of these capacities. The audit builds end-setting (what should I delegate?). The delegation template builds reason-giving (why these success criteria?). The standup builds contestability (was that output actually right?). Capacity dissolution is the cost of skipping practice. The five capacities give us a diagnostic: if a pipeline doesn't exercise at least two of them, it's building dependency, not capability.

**Source:** Yao — "When AI Does the Work, What Is Learning For? Post-Instrumental Learning and the Risk of Capacity Dissolution" (arXiv:2607.28041, AAAI/ACM AIES 2026)

## Concept 3: Asymmetric Responsibility — The AI Bears No Commitments

**What it is:** Drawing on Wittgenstein, Luhmann, and Brandom, Enzo Fenoglio argues that human-LLM interaction is **structurally asymmetric** along three dimensions:
- **Correctness is enforced exclusively by the receiver** — the AI doesn't check its own work
- **Accountability is borne by human participants alone** — the AI doesn't stand behind its output
- **Practical standing of any output depends entirely on human uptake** — the AI's words have force only when a human grants it

These conditions are structural, hold independently of capability, and remain unchanged as models get more powerful. The AI's output circulates communicatively without the system undertaking commitments, bearing entitlements, or performing the assessment on which discursive standing depends.

**Why it matters:** This is the mental model shift that underlies every practice exercise. When you audit your prompts, you're taking back the correctness check. When you define success criteria before delegating, you're exercising the accountability the AI can't bear. When you hold a daily standup, you're performing the assessment the system can't perform. The operator treats the AI as a conversation partner. The orchestrator treats it as a tool that produces output — and knows that all the standing, accountability, and correctness happens on the human side.

**Source:** Fenoglio — "Asymmetric Communication: Large Language Models and Language Games" (arXiv:2607.28137, July 2026)

## Concept 4: Structured Reflection — Metacognition as a Trainable Skill

**What it is:** An intervention study (N=29) introduced creative task cards to help CS1 programming students regulate their emotional and cognitive experience during assignments. Three findings with direct practice implications:
- **Breaks + peer reflection** helped students process feelings of being stuck — particularly when they could physically leave the classroom
- **Drawing** helped students externalize and reflect on cognitive overload better than writing about it
- **Breaking work into smaller steps** increased self-efficacy — students who decomposed the task felt more capable of completing it

**Why it matters:** The operator→orchestrator shift is cognitively loaded. You're learning new mental models (delegation, pipeline design, quality assessment) while also doing your actual work. Structured reflection — the kind these task cards provide — isn't a nice-to-have. It's the mechanism that prevents cognitive overload from becoming cognitive surrender. The specific techniques (breaks, drawing, decomposition) are free, require no tools, and take under 5 minutes. They're the metacognitive layer that makes the rest of the practice section stick.

**Source:** Ford, Li, Van Koningsbruggen — "Creative Task Cards for Reflection, Self-Efficiency and Self-Regulation in CS1 Introductory Programming" (arXiv:2607.27863, RiCE Workshop, July 2026)

# ④ For the Operator (Actionable TODAY)

- **Before you evaluate AI output, solve one yourself.** Pick any task you'd normally delegate. Spend 3 minutes producing your own version — even a rough one — before looking at what the AI produced. The difference between your version and the AI's is where your learning lives. The N=220 study shows evaluation alone doesn't transfer. The comparison does.

- **Ask "why" about one AI decision today.** When an AI produces output, pick one specific choice it made (a source it cited, a structure it used, a claim it made) and ask it to walk through the reasoning. If you can follow it, you've built understanding. If you can't, you've found where the AI did more of the thinking than you realized.

- **Do the 30-second contribution check.** After your next AI collaboration, name one specific thing that was *your* contribution — not "I wrote the prompt" but a judgment, framing, or decision that was uniquely yours. If you can't name one, Fenoglio's framework says you're in the asymmetric trap: the AI produced output and you accepted it, but no accountability transferred. That's not delegation — it's spectating.

# ⑤ For the Orchestrator (Workflows & Patterns)

- **Add a Capacity Check to every pipeline template.** For each pipeline you run, ask: which of Yao's five capacities (end-setting, reason-giving, contestability, refusal, participation) does this pipeline exercise for me? If the answer is "none," the pipeline is building dependency, not capability. Add one step that forces you to exercise at least two — even if it adds 30 seconds.

- **The Seventh Standup Question — Asymmetric Check.** Add to your [[The Daily Standup|Daily Standup]]: *"What did I take responsibility for today that the AI couldn't have?"* Not "what did the AI do well" — what did YOU own? After a week of answers, the pattern tells you whether you're delegating tasks or abdicating responsibility. Fenoglio's framework: the AI bears no commitments. You do. The standup is where you track whether you're actually bearing them.

- **Design one "unaided round" into a pipeline this week.** Pick your most reliable pipeline. Add a step where you produce something without AI — a judgment call, a prioritization, a quality assessment — before the AI takes over. The N=220 study shows that evaluating AI output and producing your own output are different muscles. The unaided round keeps both alive.

- **Try a drawing checkpoint.** Next time you feel cognitively overloaded mid-pipeline — too many steps, too much output, can't tell if it's working — stop. Take 2 minutes. Draw what you think is happening in the pipeline. Boxes and arrows on scratch paper. The Ford et al. study found that drawing externalizes overload better than writing about it. You don't need to be good at drawing. You just need to see the shape of the problem outside your head.

# ⑦ Top Insight of the Day

**Evaluating AI output and producing your own output are different muscles — and confusing them is the most common practice mistake in the operator→orchestrator shift.** The N=220 randomized study proves this empirically: students got better homework scores evaluating AI solutions but didn't learn more. Yao's five capacities name what's at stake: end-setting, reason-giving, contestability, refusal, and participation. Fenoglio's asymmetry framework explains why: the AI bears no commitments, so practicing evaluation means practicing YOUR accountability, not the AI's correctness. The practice section of this wiki isn't about learning to use AI better. It's about learning to stand apart from AI output — and that's a skill you build, not one you already have.
