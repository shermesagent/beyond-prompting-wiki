> **Day 1 — The Shift.** Three directly read publisher abstracts, not full papers. The first proposes a framework; the other two describe one deployment and an observational study of job ads. None establishes school-district outcomes.

## ① Theme Title

**Give the work a reason, not just an AI label**

## ② Executive Summary

Before handing off a task, make its purpose visible and decide who can change it. An AI tool can be widely used without proving that every task is better; a job ad can mention AI without telling us how work actually changed. Start with one checkable task and keep the human decision in view.

## ③ Concepts Discovered

- **The goal card:** State the outcome, a boundary, proof and the person who can revise the goal. A [goals-framework preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08757) argues that a prompt can lose sight of the broader purpose; this is a proposal, not a tested improvement. → [[What Is Beyond Prompting]], [[Intent Scaffolding]].
- **Reusable work with a source trail:** A [single-system health deployment](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08672) reports training tied to access, cited record search and saved tasks. That suggests a design question—not a proven cause of adoption: can people repeat an approved task and inspect its basis? → [[Why This Matters]].
- **Tasks over AI badges:** A [job-ad analysis](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09870) associates AI exposure with modest changes in advertised responsibilities, even as explicit AI mentions rise. Ads are not direct observation of employees. → [[Why This Matters]], [[Task Decomposition]].

## ⑤ For the Operator

Choose one harmless task, such as a public event announcement. On paper write `OUTCOME / MUST NOT CHANGE / HOW I CHECK / WHO DECIDES`. Ask AI for a draft only; compare its dates and claims with the original public source yourself. No student, patient or personnel information in an unapproved tool.

## ⑥ For the Orchestrator

Pick one approved recurring task. Save the goal card, public input, output and check steps as a reusable template; assign the final decision to a named person. After two uses, compare the time spent reviewing and the errors found with the old process. Do not infer improved outcomes from usage counts alone.
