# ③ Concepts Added to Glossary

## New Entry 1: Capacity Dissolution

**The concept:** When AI produces outputs through which institutions recognize competence — essays, code, reports, decisions — five human capacities erode: end-setting, reason-giving, contestability, refusal/revision, and participation. Named by Kai Yao (AAAI/ACM AIES 2026), this is the hidden cost of output delegation that works.

**Why the glossary needed it:** Capacity Dissolution now appears in [[Overassistance]], [[The Coaching Stance]], [[Audit Your Prompts]], [[First Delegation]], [[The Daily Standup]], and the practice section overview. It's the framework behind nearly every practice exercise in this wiki. Without a glossary entry, someone encountering "end-setting" or "contestability" for the first time has to read the full concept page — which is the right move for depth, but wrong for quick orientation. The glossary entry gives the 30-second version with a link to go deeper.

## New Entry 2: Cognitive Fixed Cost

**The concept:** The upfront concentration of cognitive labor that transforms the cost structure of every subsequent instance of a task. Pay once — by designing the workflow, building the review architecture, defining the output format — and every future run costs near-zero marginal cognitive effort. From Li & Cao (arXiv:2607.25240, July 2026).

**Why the glossary needed it:** Cognitive Fixed Cost is referenced in [[SOP]], [[Memory]], [[From Prompt to Pipeline]], [[The School District Shift]], [[Small Business Automation]], and the economics overview. It's the mechanism that explains *why* delegation compounds — and without a glossary entry, it reads as jargon. The entry translates "fixed cost" from economics into plain language: the orchestrator invests once, the operator pays per instance.

## New Entry 3: The Blank Box Problem

**The concept:** The default AI interface — an empty text box with no menus, no templates, no guide rails — demands recall from every user every time. It keeps you at the execution level because you're too busy remembering what to type to think about what's worth doing. Named from Shneiderman's control-panel alternative (arXiv:2607.21598, July 2026).

**Why the glossary needed it:** The Blank Box Problem appears in [[What Is Beyond Prompting]], [[The Operator Mindset]], [[The Orchestrator Mindset]], [[From Prompt to Pipeline]], and [[Build a Tiny Pipeline]]. It's the starting-point insight — the first thing someone new to the wiki needs to understand about why the blank chat window is not neutral. The glossary entry validates the reader's experience ("you're not bad at prompting — the interface is designed for recall") and gives them an immediate action (build a template).

## New Entry 4: Failure-Path Preservation

**The concept:** The property of a knowledge system that preserves what *didn't* work — dead ends, failed approaches, wrong assumptions, corrected claims — alongside what did. Without it, every agent deployment starts from scratch, doomed to repeat mistakes because the record of prior failures evaporated. From Saboia Moreira & Sweet (arXiv:2607.24759, July 2026).

**Why the glossary needed it:** Failure-Path Preservation connects to [[Cognitive Fixed Cost]], [[Memory]], [[Delegation Thinking]], [[Small Business Automation]], and [[The School District Shift]]. It's the substrate property that makes the orchestrator's investments compound rather than evaporate. The glossary entry translates "sociotechnical property" into a habit anyone can start: write down what didn't work and why.

# ④ Existing Glossary Entries Deepened

## Updated: Memory — Added the Cognitive Fixed Cost Connection

Memory was already one of the stronger glossary entries, covering session memory, persistent memory, the weakest relay principle, and memory as infrastructure. The addition: a new paragraph connecting memory to cognitive fixed cost — **the orchestrator pays the cognitive fixed cost upfront, and memory is what stores the payoff.** Every SOP, every saved template, every preference the agent remembers is the stored return on the upfront investment. Without memory, every cognitive fixed cost evaporates. The memory entry now explicitly frames memory as the compounding mechanism for orchestration investments. Also added the blank box connection: memory solves the blank box by carrying forward context so every interaction doesn't start from zero.

## Updated: SOP — Added the Cognitive Fixed Cost Connection

The SOP entry was already solid, describing the four-part structure (Inputs, Outputs, Constraints, Checkpoints) and the EvoSOP lifecycle. The addition: an explicit frame connecting SOPs to cognitive fixed cost — **an SOP is the stored payoff of a cognitive fixed cost investment.** When you write an SOP, you're making the investment visible, reusable, and improvable. The entry now distinguishes between the one-off delegation (operator level) and the SOP (the investment made permanent). Also added: the Blank Box countermeasure — having a library of SOPs means you never face the blank box for recurring tasks.

## Updated: Oversight — Added Capacity Dissolution as the Second Check

The Oversight entry already covered the adversarial/passive/collaborative modes and overassistance. The addition: a second dimension to oversight — **checking not just correctness, but whether the AI dissolved any capacity in the process.** The existing entry asks "is this correct?" The new paragraph asks: "Did this interaction exercise my end-setting? My reason-giving? My contestability?" Oversight that only checks output correctness is half-blind. Oversight that also checks capacity preservation is the full orchestrator stance. Added Yao's five capacities as the checklist, with a practical one-question addition to any review: "Did I get better at anything during this interaction?"

# ⑤ For the Operator (Actionable TODAY)

- **Find one concept you don't fully understand.** Scan the glossary. If you hit one where the definition doesn't click, follow the wikilink to the full concept page. The glossary is your on-ramp. Today's additions — Capacity Dissolution, Cognitive Fixed Cost, The Blank Box Problem — each have a 30-second definition and a practice exercise you can do immediately.

- **Save one template today.** The Blank Box Problem is real: the empty chat window asks you to remember everything from scratch. Pick one task you've done more than twice this week. Open a blank document. Write down: the format you want, the quality standard, the constraints. Save it. Next time that task comes up, paste the template instead of starting from zero. You just paid your first cognitive fixed cost.

- **Write down one thing that didn't work.** Failure-Path Preservation starts with one entry. When did you try something with AI that went wrong? What did you try? What happened? Put it in a note somewhere you'll find it. The operator thinks "I'll remember." The orchestrator knows they won't.

# ⑥ For the Orchestrator (Workflows & Patterns)

- **Audit your glossary against your pipelines.** For each pipeline you run regularly, scan the glossary. Which terms does it depend on? Which ones does your team understand? If someone new joined tomorrow, could they read the glossary entries for Agent, Delegation, SOP, and Memory and understand what your pipeline does? If not, which entries need examples from your actual workflow added? The glossary is your onboarding document. Treat it like one.

- **Add a Capacity Check to your SOP library.** Pick your most-used SOP. Add one line to the constraints section: "Preserve at least two of the five capacities (end-setting, reason-giving, contestability, refusal, participation)." When you run the SOP next, do the 30-second Capacity Check from [[Capacity Dissolution]]. If the SOP passes three consecutive checks, it's a healthy pipeline. If it consistently fails, it's building dependency. Update or retire.

- **Build one "failure log" into your delegations.** The next time you delegate something and it doesn't go right, don't just fix the output — save the failure path. What did you ask for? What did you get? What was wrong? What did you change? Put it where your SOP lives. Next time you or someone else delegates the same kind of task, the failure log prevents a repeat. That's failure-path preservation in practice — and it's free.
