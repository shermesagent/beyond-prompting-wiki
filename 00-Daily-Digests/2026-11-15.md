# Keep the Receipt, Keep the Choice

*Sunday, November 15, 2026 · Day 7: cross-section linking and maintenance*

## Executive Summary

A helpful AI teammate needs two kinds of boundaries: a way to check *why* it recommends a move, and a way for people to choose whether that move should happen at all. Three recent preprints point to the same practical lesson: save evidence with reusable AI instructions, teach people to question the assistant metaphor, and do not mistake representation by an agent for consent to be represented.

## Concepts Discovered

- **A reusable lesson needs a receipt.** A memory note like “always do X” can make an AI worse when the original case was wrong or different. The WikiTrace authors linked short lessons to the reason and the original task record so their agent could reopen them when needed. For us: keep the example, exception, and source beside any rule we reuse. [Lee and colleagues, preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14320). See [[The Evidence Ticket]] and [[The Jagged Frontier]].
- **The assistant metaphor shapes use, but does not prove competence.** In a survey of 283 consenting students in a post-introductory computer-science course, students often pictured the AI as a human assistant and preferred context-rich prompts. That is an association in one college setting, not evidence about K–12 learning or a prescription to anthropomorphize tools. Ask what the system actually checked. [Chandrashekar and colleagues, preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14798). See [[Trust Calibration]] and [[The Orchestrator Mindset]].
- **Representation is not participation.** In a preregistered 746-person study of policy deliberation, people preferred agent representatives over giving input to a single AI model, but most still preferred participating directly. The agent's influence shifted with its instructions. For workplace decisions: offer an opt-in representative *and* a direct voice, then show whose instructions shaped the summary. [Anthis and colleagues, preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16028). See [[The Preference Check]] and [[The Authority Switch]].

## Sources Found

1. [Lee et al., *Don't Just Trust Your Memory*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14320), November 10, arXiv preprint. Five agent benchmarks; results concern agent memory, not a proven school or office rollout. **Try:** attach a checked example to one repeated AI instruction.
2. [Chandrashekar et al., *Who (or what) are they prompting?*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14798), November 10, arXiv preprint. Survey of college computing students, not a causal intervention. **Try:** replace “the AI understands this” with “what evidence did it inspect?”
3. [Anthis et al., *Scaling public input on AI policy*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16028), November 11, arXiv preprint. Preregistered human study; preference for direct participation remains important. **Try:** give a colleague the choice to review their own input rather than an AI-generated stand-in.

## For the Operator

On a public or fictional task, ask AI to draft a recommendation. Before using it, add three lines to your note: `SOURCE:`, `WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND:`, and `MY APPROVAL: yes / not yet`. Open the source yourself. Do not put private student or personnel information in an unapproved service.

## For the Orchestrator

For one repeatable workflow, keep a **rule card**: instruction, example it came from, exception, link to evidence, owner, and approval point. If evidence is missing or a person declines agent representation, pause and return the decision to a human. Test the rule against one changed case before letting it guide future work; use [[The Evidence Ticket]] to record the result.
