# Check the Claim, Not Just the Output

## Executive Summary

A busy AI workflow is not necessarily a trustworthy one. Today's three research preprints point to three separate things worth checking: who uses an AI tool, whether its output actually addresses the original task, and whether a person approves the work before it travels farther. These are ideas for small, safe trials—not evidence that any particular school district has deployed them successfully.

## Concepts Discovered

1. **Use is not impact.** An interview study at one software organization counted 98 scored records across eleven teams; self-reported use varied by team, and the authors caution that different question wording in one team makes the pooled rate hard to interpret. This matters because a login count cannot tell you whether the work was correct or policy-compliant. **Try:** next to any adoption count, track one completed output and its reviewer. [Verì, *Uneven Self-Reported AI Use*, preprint (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14154).
2. **A helpful drafting tool still needs a subject-matter check.** An educational platform grouped assessment questions by the skills they assess and supported question drafting. A small, partly researcher-assisted evaluation with eight instructional designers measured perceived usability—not student learning or district-scale effectiveness. **Try:** use a publicly released question and have a teacher compare the tool's suggested skill to what the student must actually do. [Wei, Sutton & Carvalho, *Perspicacious*, preprint (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13549).
3. **A passed check can verify the wrong thing.** A proposed research-audit framework distinguishes validating an artifact from confirming it faithfully represents the original claim; it places a human decision at a defined point. **Try:** put the exact claim, source passage, reviewer and decision in the same record. Applying it to district content is a suggested analogy, not a result demonstrated by the paper. [Eigner, Süli & Hollós, *Trust, but verify, audit and attribute*, preprint (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13042).

## For the Operator

Choose one *public* document you already know. Ask an approved tool for a short summary. Highlight one consequential sentence, reopen the original passage, and mark that sentence **supported**, **needs revision**, or **do not use**. You have practiced verification without changing tools or uploading private data.

## For the Orchestrator

Run a one-item, low-stakes pilot: **public input → AI draft → source-to-claim check → named human approval → released output**. Keep a small log with the source link, one disputed claim, the decision and the person who made it. Measure accepted work, not merely tool use. For student work, personnel records or confidential district materials, use only district-approved systems and existing privacy review; this public-material exercise is not permission to transfer them.
