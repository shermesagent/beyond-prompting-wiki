# Daily Digest — 2026-10-14

> **Day 3 — Real World.** Three newly announced human-centered computing preprints, read from primary arXiv abstracts and publication metadata. The practical steps below are suggestions, not interventions proven in Farmersville ISD. No private records were ingested.

## ① Theme Title

**A Review Needs a Trail—and a Person Who Can Use It**

## ② Executive Summary

Delegation can increase finished work while making errors harder to spot. Today's studies point to a more useful measure of a working AI workflow: can a person get from the summary back to the evidence, explain the choice, and keep doing that review week after week? Build the review job as carefully as the agent job.

## ③ Concepts Discovered

- **Finished is not understood.** In a [20-professional think-aloud study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.14660), heavier delegation on a data-science hiring assessment went with more completed work *and* more invalid solutions; experts more often broke the work into pieces and checked results. For [[From Prompt to Pipeline]], add one independent input check and a plain-language explanation before approving a draft. This small study does not estimate an organization-wide error rate.
- **A cue needs a route back.** [ArguScale](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.15319) links summaries of simultaneous classroom conversations to the relevant transcript and audio. In a 20-person evaluation, participants more efficiently and accurately found collaboration cues than in comparison conditions. For [[The School District Shift]], use a cue to locate the underlying contribution—not as a verdict about a student. Audio and transcripts are sensitive; the five-minute exercise uses a public or invented-by-you, non-student example, not classroom recordings.
- **Review has a workload, too.** A [three-month study of eight tutors](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.16410) overseeing an AI learning assistant found repetitive checking, unclear effects of interventions and weak connection with learners and peers made oversight harder; interface changes had mixed success. For [[Small Business Automation]], ask reviewers whether their corrections change the next run and whether the cadence is sustainable. The study is in tutoring, not a trial of bakery operations.

| [Faas et al., *What UI Design Can Do For “Good” Oversight Work*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.16410) | Three-month action research with eight tutors; preprint submitted Oct 12 | Ask whether a reviewer can see what changed after their correction. |

*Evidence boundary:* All three are preprints. Their samples, tasks and methods differ. None measures a Farmersville ISD deployment, proves a particular AI product safe or justifies placing identifiable student conversations in an unapproved service.

## ⑤ For the Operator

Take one public, non-sensitive AI draft you already have. Underline a claim, find its original source, and explain in one sentence why you would keep or change it. If you cannot reach the source, mark the claim **unverified** rather than treating fluent wording as proof. See [[The Review-First Pattern]].

## ⑥ For the Orchestrator

Trial a **draft-only** review card on public material: `Claim or cue → source excerpt → human decision → correction → result on next run`. Ask the reviewer to find one planted mistake and say how long the check takes; adjust scope or frequency if the review cannot be sustained. Keep any student, staff, customer, audio or transcript records in approved systems only. [[The Evidence Interface]] and [[The Observability Gap]] explain why the source needs to travel with the output.
