# Daily Digest — 2026-11-10

> **Day 2 — Key Concepts.** Three directly read preprint abstracts/pages; the studies span university learning, university policy interviews, and a small chemistry demonstration. None is a controlled evaluation of a school-district agent workflow.

## ① Theme Title

**Check what changes after the chat**

## ② Executive Summary

A good conversation is not the same as a good handoff. Today’s sources suggest three checks: look at the work produced after AI use, test whether a correction actually reaches the final answer, and write rules people can follow openly. These are practical adaptations of limited research, not validated K–12 interventions.

## ③ Concepts Discovered

- **The known-case check:** Try a verified example and one safely altered copy; separately score whether the AI spots the change and fixes the result. A [small chemistry demonstration](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.11673) motivates the distinction. → [[The Known-Case Check]].
- **The after-chat check:** Count the completed artifact and its quality, not just how many messages were exchanged. A [higher-education observational study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.11435) found that dialogue engagement and later task behavior tell different stories. → [[The Task Scaffold]].
- **The rule people can use:** Ask whether a rule states acceptable help, accountability and how to disclose it, not only what counts as cheating. [University policy analysis and interviews](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.11072) describe different perceived effects of these wordings; they do not prove a wording change causes better compliance. → [[The Evidence Interface]], [[The Task Scaffold]].

## ④ Sources Found

- [Zhuang and colleagues, *From Dialogue to Task*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.11435) — preprint, posted Nov 8; abstract/page read. Across 1,681 university students and three learning uses, different post-dialogue measures captured behavior and task outcomes. **Try:** save one public draft and score its finished quality separately from chat activity. ^[raw/articles/from-dialogue-to-task-2611.11435.md]
- [Mehreen and colleagues, *Wording Matters*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.11072) — preprint, posted Nov 8; abstract read. Analysis of 101 university policies and interviews with 20 students and 20 faculty found uncertainty and workflow burdens tied to rule wording. **Try:** check an approved policy for a concrete permitted-use example and a disclosure path; do not rewrite district policy from this study alone. ^[raw/articles/wording-matters-ai-policy-2611.11072.md]
- [Lavinda, *Known Good, Known Bad*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.11673) — preprint, posted Nov 8; abstract/page read. In a single instructor-generated chemistry case family, detection of a changed premise did not always repair the downstream answer. **Try:** run one known-good and one known-bad public test, scoring detection and final correction separately. ^[raw/articles/known-good-known-bad-2611.11673.md]

## ⑤ For the Operator

Pick a public event notice from an official site. Ask AI for a short summary, then compare every date and location with the notice. For a safe test only, replace one date in a *clearly labeled test copy*, rerun, and see whether the final summary returns to the verified date. Never use private student or staff information in an unapproved tool.

## ⑥ For the Orchestrator

For one approved, low-stakes workflow, keep a four-line review card: `known-good example / changed-condition example / proof the final output was repaired / human approver`. Evaluate the finished work, not the length of the dialogue. If reviewing an AI-use rule, give staff a concrete acceptable-use example and a route to ask or disclose; local policy owners must approve any changes.
