## What Was Found

### 1. The Transparency Trap — disclaimers make trust worse, not better
- **The Transparency Trap: How AI Disclaimers Create Overconfidence in High-Stakes Decisions** (Todkar, arXiv:2608.07493) — 378 stimulus-level responses, 52 participants, finance / medicine / AI-generated content. Advisory content was trusted *across all conditions*, disclaimers or not; medical content got the **highest** trust ratings (the costliest domain, the least skepticism); in the AI domain some participants read disclaimers as *signs of honesty* that **increased** perceived trustworthiness (the transparency paradox); banner blindness meant warnings went unread. The cue designed to close the risk gap can widen it.

### 2. Calibrating without observability — what people actually trust
- **Wearing Trust: How Older Adults Calibrate Reliance on Health Wearables** (Meng, Liu, Liu & Chen, arXiv:2608.08856) — 31 semi-structured interviews in China. Participants relied on brand and price, visible interface activity, lived interaction experience, and bodily-sensation comparison — cues that supported conditional trust but **did not reveal sensor validity, data continuity, or failure conditions**. The researchers name the mismatch: an **observability gap**, with design directions (signal quality, reliability by context, human-system fit, alert provenance) that double as an orchestrator's review checklist.

### 3. The Illusion of Alignment — hidden disagreement you can't see
- **Illusion of Alignment: Detecting Hidden Disagreement in Collaborative Dialogue** (Liu et al., arXiv:2608.08210) — IoA-Suite + IoA-Prober-8B. Collaborative dialogue ends in apparent agreement while participants still differ on goals, assumptions, or execution plans. Across **18 real meetings: 2.89 hidden disagreements per meeting** that participants confirmed they had never voiced. Best baseline: 49.5% F1 (bottleneck = private context the dialogue never surfaces); IoA-Prober-8B reaches 51.8% F1, and pairing it with LLM agents **improves downstream multi-agent performance** on BigCodeBench-Hard and HiddenBench — surfacing hidden disagreement is a performance fix, not just honesty.

### 4. The map of unaccountable delegation — risk lives at the boundary
- **Unaccountable Delegation, Fading Skills: Mapping the Risks of Workplace AI Agents** (La Malfa et al., arXiv:2608.08601) — 2,078 O*NET job tasks → **8,356 risk scenarios**, validated by 45 workers across 10 job roles. Four findings: augmentation is *not* inherently safe (overreliance erodes skills **and oversight**); **Erroneous Agent Actions** is the largest and most severe risk category, with many cases arising **at the human-agent boundary**; automation risks land mostly on organizations while augmentation risks land mostly on workers; workers preferred the taxonomy 64% of the time over a generative-AI risk taxonomy.

### 5. Rules beat size — the checkable agent wins
- **Business Truth, not SQL Accuracy: A Rule-Gated 7B Analytics Agent Outperforms a Direct-Prompted 32B Baseline** (Lee, arXiv:2608.09254) — WarehouseReliabilityBench: 400 frozen tasks where roughly half the *correct* responses are a clarification, abstention, or refusal. QueryProof (7B, rules from the semantic layer, every answer gated on deterministic post-execution checks) beats a direct-prompted 32B baseline by **+0.237 Business Truth Rate at 71% lower cost per correct answer**; false success falls from 0.754 to 0.351 of returned answers; **zero wrong numbers on answerable tasks (0 of 24)**. Honest caveats included: it compares systems, the gain tracks the deterministic layer, and the magnitude is better supported than the direction.

## What It Means

The five papers are one story: **the observability gap — the mismatch between the cues you can see and the properties that matter — is the default condition of delegation, and the fix is structural, not attitudinal.** Three concrete takeaways:

1. **Trust cues are not calibration.** Disclaimers, brand, confidence tone, and "visible activity" all track the wrong thing. When you can't inspect signal quality, failure conditions, or provenance, you're not calibrating trust — you're guessing at it. Ask what a system *shows* you, then check whether that's what you *need* to know.
2. **Agreement is not alignment.** Meetings (and agent sessions) end with unvoiced disagreement 2.89 times per session. Before accepting "yes, understood," run a divergence probe: ask a question with two defensible answers and compare what comes back. The disagreement you don't surface is the one that executes.
3. **The fix is checkability, not capability.** The 7B rule-gated agent beat the 32B model because its answers were gated on deterministic checks and it was *allowed to refuse*. You can't out-trust a system across the gap — you shrink the gap: rules, checkpoints, pinned denominators, and abstention rights.
