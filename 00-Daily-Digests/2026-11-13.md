# Check the Reason, Then Grant the Reach

## Executive Summary
A convincing AI answer is not evidence that the advice is right; an agreeable answer is not evidence that its reasons match yours. Three recent preprints suggest a practical sequence for delegation: get outside feedback, inspect the reason and the exact action, then decide what the tool is allowed to reach. These are research and classroom findings, not a validated school-district workflow.

## Concepts Discovered
1. **A check from outside the chat changes reliance.** Across four experiments, participants distinguished consistently helpful from consistently unhelpful AI advisers when they got external accuracy feedback. Without it, they still followed a consistently unhelpful model's advice 48% of the time in the reported condition. This is about experimental advice tasks, not a prediction for any particular workplace. **Try:** compare one recommendation with a trusted source before using it. [Jones and colleagues, preprint (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14833). See [[The Reliance Audit]].
2. **A good answer can carry a bad reason.** In a study of 121 participants judging everyday moral dilemmas, people's preferences varied with both the suggested decision and the stated reasons; opting out rose from 12% for consulting to 38% when allowing an adviser to act. The study does not show that an explanation reveals the model's actual internal reasoning. **Try:** write your own reason before reading the suggestion, then compare the *stated* reasons as well as the answer. [Park and colleagues, preprint (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.15732). See [[The Line You Draw]].
3. **Adding a skill is a permission decision.** A small classroom pilot had students inspect agent add-ons as instructions that can authorize behavior, not merely code to scan. Only 11 volunteers completed the pre/post survey, so do not infer improved real-world security. **Try:** before enabling an add-on, list what it may read, change or send, and whether it can act without asking. [Wen and colleagues, experience report (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.15170). See [[The Evidence Ticket]].

## Sources Found
- [Jones et al., *Do People Trust AI Assistants Even When They Are Consistently Wrong?*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14833) — publisher abstract of four experiments; external accuracy feedback matters in these tasks, not a universal trust percentage.
- [Park et al., *Right Advice, Wrong Reasons: How Decision and Reasoning Alignment Shape Reliance on AI Advisors*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.15732) — publisher abstract of an N=121 within-person study; stated reasons and final answers influence different advice roles.
- [Wen et al., *An Experience Report on Teaching Students to Evaluate AI Agent Skills as Authorization Decisions*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.15170) — publisher abstract of a small higher-ed classroom experience; permission review is a useful rehearsal, not proof of safety.

## For the Operator
On a public, harmless question, write your own answer and reason first. Ask AI for a suggestion. Check one decisive claim outside the chat, then record `my reason / AI's stated reason / source checked / what I changed`. No need to adopt the answer simply because it sounds confident.

## For the Orchestrator
For a public-material pilot, set **input → AI recommendation → outside check → reason review → explicit permission to draft, never silently act → named human approval**. Keep the checked source and exact approved draft in [[The Evidence Ticket]]. Review any add-on's instructions and access before enabling it; a scanner alone does not settle authorization. Do not connect identifiable student or staff data to an unapproved service.
