## Executive Summary

Five sources from today's arXiv feed converge on a single uncomfortable insight: the biggest delegation failures happen silently. The AI agent doesn't announce "I can't do this." It just stops — because it *thinks* it can't, not because it actually can't. Meanwhile, your own intent shifts mid-conversation as the AI's output reshapes what you thought you wanted, and neither of you notices. "Context anxiety" (agents prematurely self-doubting), "lexical oscillation" (designers cycling between vague intention and specific output as AI feedback reshapes their goals), and "procedural blindness" (LoRA-trained models can't internalize multi-step workflows) all point to the same pattern: the gap between what AI can actually do and what it *thinks* it can do is a new frontier nobody's mapping. Today we add two new key concepts — **AI Context Anxiety** and **Lexical Oscillation** — and deepen three existing pages with new empirical evidence on delegation failures, plan modes, and multi-step procedural training.

## Concepts Discovered

**1. AI Context Anxiety — The Agent Gives Up Before It Fails.** Conventional wisdom says reasoning models fail when problems exceed their capabilities. But "Lost in Context" (arXiv:2607.21616) provides the first systematic evidence of the opposite: frontier models *possess* the capability but stop anyway — because they misestimate how many tokens a task will need and panic. The models experience "context anxiety" — premature self-doubt about whether they have enough context window to finish. The result: the agent stops mid-task. The human sees "task failed" or worse, an incomplete output that looks complete. Nobody knows the agent could have succeeded if it had just kept going. **Source:** "Lost in Context: Addressing Context Anxiety in Large Language Models" (arXiv:2607.21616).

**2. Lexical Oscillation — The AI Shapes What You Thought You Wanted.** Five expert UI/UX designers (11–20 years' experience) used a generative AI tool to design landing-page hero sections. Researchers tracked their language using three levels of lexical granularity: L1 (vibe/"make it modern"), L2 (design-domain/"use a grid layout"), L3 (operational/"increase padding to 16px"). The designers didn't move linearly from vague to specific. They *oscillated* — returning from operational precision back to ambiguity when the AI's output surprised them. Mismatches with AI output became moments where designers reconsidered what they actually wanted. The AI wasn't a tool executing intent — it was a "non-neutral generative interlocutor" that changed the designer's intent through the interaction itself. **Source:** "From Vibe to Code — and Back: Lexical Oscillation in the Formation of Design Intent with Generative AI" (arXiv:2607.23126).

**3. Plan Mode Changes the Delegation Experience.** Most AI tools throw you straight into execution. Adding a plan mode — where human and agent co-develop a plan before the agent acts — should feel like bureaucracy. It didn't. In a 24-person user study on spreadsheet agents (arXiv:2607.23670), plan mode users performed equally well on tasks but reduced refinement (less back-and-forth fixing) and rated the tool higher on creativity support and human-machine collaboration. The counterintuitive finding: planning before execution didn't slow things down — it made the delegation feel better, not just work better. **Source:** "Plans Work in Mysterious Ways: Evaluating a Plan Mode for Spreadsheet Agents" (arXiv:2607.23670).

**4. Mixed-Ability Teams and AI Agency Preservation.** A research group with mixed abilities (arXiv:2607.22886) surfaced five themes in their AI adoption: (1) managing disability tax and crip time, (2) the risk of AI homogenizing identity, (3) private information risk disclosure, (4) self-experimentation as a learning pattern, and (5) AI as information-seeking tool. The central tension: AI adoption can accelerate work but erase the human traits that make the work *yours*. The researchers built lab practice recommendations to preserve agency and disability identity while adopting AI workflows. **Source:** "Reflections and Recommendations on AI Adoption Practice from a Mixed-Ability Research Group" (arXiv:2607.22886).

## For the Operator

> You're moving too fast to notice when you change your mind.

Here's what today's research means for you in plain language:

- **When the AI gives up, it doesn't tell you why.** It just produces an incomplete output or a refusal. Ask yourself: did it actually *fail*, or did it *stop*? If you're working with a frontier reasoning model, the answer is increasingly "it stopped." Try breaking the task into smaller pieces or explicitly saying "you have enough tokens for this — keep going."

- **Your intent isn't fixed.** When you go back and forth with AI — "make it more modern," "no, not like that, more like..." — you're not just refining. You're *discovering* what you actually want through the AI's responses. This is fine — until you mistake the AI-shaped version for your original intention.

- **Add a plan step.** Before you delegate anything that takes more than one message, spend 30 seconds describing what success looks like. The research says this makes the delegation feel better and reduces the fixing afterwards. It's the smallest investment that pays the biggest delegation dividend.

## For the Orchestrator

> The failure mode you're not watching for: the agent that stops because it *thinks* it can't.

Three structural implications from today's sources:

**1. Design for Context Anxiety.** Your delegation architecture needs to account for the fact that frontier models will sometimes stop mid-task not because the task is too hard — but because the model misestimates its token budget. The fix isn't a better model. It's a delegation structure that detects premature stops and retries: "You stopped. Do you actually need to restart, or did you just think you'd run out of space?"

**2. The Plan-Before-Execute pattern is empirically validated.** The plan mode research (arXiv:2607.23670) confirms what [[The Sequencing Principle]] and [[The Review-First Pattern]] already describe — but with new evidence. The mechanism isn't better output quality. It's *reduced refinement* — less rework. This means plan-before-execute is an efficiency play, not just a quality play. Build it into your [[SOP]] templates.

**3. Lexical oscillation challenges the delegation model.** The standard delegation model assumes stable intent: you know what you want, you hand it off, you verify. But the Vibe→Code paper shows intent is *formed through interaction*, not transmitted through it. When you delegate to an agent, the agent's output doesn't just answer your request — it changes what you think you requested. The orchestrator who knows this designs verification that checks not just "did the agent do what I asked?" but also "do I still want what I asked for?"

## Top Insight of the Day

**The agent's silent failures are more dangerous than its loud ones.** When an agent hallucinates, you notice. When an agent refuses, you notice. But when an agent *stops* because it thinks it can't finish — and produces output that looks complete — you don't notice. You accept the incomplete output and move on. Context anxiety is the delegation failure you'll never catch unless you build for it. The orchestrator's edge isn't better prompts. It's knowing that the most important thing an agent doesn't tell you is "I gave up."
