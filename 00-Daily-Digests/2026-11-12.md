# Keep the Choice, Test the Label

## Executive Summary

Moving beyond prompting need not mean handing over every decision. Three research preprints suggest a better starting point: ask what people want to keep, test whether AI labels stay steady, and keep the exact labeling rule next to the result. These are prompts for low-stakes, public-material trials—not evidence that any school district has adopted or validated these approaches.

## Concepts Discovered

1. **Delegation is a choice about authority, not just workload.** In a survey of 289 U.S. adults with prior AI experience, automation preferences varied by scenario; open responses often favored substantial AI help with a person retaining oversight or final authority. This is a survey of hypothetical situations, not observed workplace behavior. **Try:** ask which step someone would gladly hand off and which decision they want to keep. [Wheeler and colleagues, *What to Delegate, What to Preserve*, preprint (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09730). See [[The Engagement Gap]].
2. **A stable-looking score can hide changing answers.** In a UK public-policy classification experiment, eight configurations had similar overall accuracy (68.2–70.3%), but 24.3% of 1,200 questions received different labels across unchanged repeats. This result belongs to that experiment, not to student assessment. **Try:** repeat a handful of public examples and compare the labels with a human-checked reference before treating a dashboard as reliable. [Yorke, *Beyond accuracy*, preprint (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06655). See [[The Fairness Dashboard Trap]].
3. **A label needs its rule.** Across six social-science concepts and three models, substituting a competing definition changed labels more, on average, than rephrasing the same definition. **Try:** keep the exact rule beside every label; when a rule changes, compare the old and new results. This study did not evaluate a K–12 risk tool. [Mamaev and colleagues, *Measuring Definition Sensitivity*, preprint (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13339). See [[The Rule Capture Problem]].

## Sources Found

- [Wheeler et al., *What to Delegate, What to Preserve*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09730) — publisher abstract of a scenario-based survey; useful for asking about preferred human authority, not predicting a team's actual behavior. [[The Engagement Gap]].
- [Yorke, *Beyond accuracy*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06655) — publisher abstract of a UK public-policy labeling study; useful for testing repeatability and human-review routing, not estimating district product performance. [[The Fairness Dashboard Trap]].
- [Mamaev et al., *Measuring Definition Sensitivity*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13339) — publisher abstract of a social-science annotation experiment; useful for keeping definitions with classifications, not for claiming that a school tool is biased. [[The Fairness Dashboard Trap]].

## For the Operator

Choose a *public, harmless* document and one proposed AI task. Before opening a tool, write: “Let AI draft ___; I keep the decision to ___.” If it would assign a label, write down exactly what that label means. You can do the whole exercise on paper; do not put student or personnel data into an unapproved tool.

## For the Orchestrator

For one approved, low-stakes pilot, set up **public input → named rule → AI suggestion → repeat check against a human-reviewed example → named person decides**. Record a changed label and the route to correction, not just the average score. Ask a participant what they want to retain and use [[The Absent Person Test]] to identify anyone affected who did not get that choice. Keep consequential decisions human-run unless privacy, accuracy and review requirements are met.
