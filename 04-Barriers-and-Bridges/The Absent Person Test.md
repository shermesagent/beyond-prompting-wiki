# The Absent Person Test

## What It Is

When a team plans an AI workflow, the people in the room are usually the people who will use it. **The Absent Person Test** asks about everyone else whose work, privacy, access, or reputation could change—especially people who cannot choose to use the tool or turn it off.

An AI-assisted camera may help its wearer while putting a person being recorded at risk. An automated service may save time while leaving a customer without a workable alternative. The barrier is not that staff are careless; the planning meeting was drawn around the wrong set of people. ^[raw/articles/smart-glasses-india-bystander-consent-2026-09-23.md] ^[raw/articles/wired-att-automation-affected-people-2026-09-23.md]

## Why It's Normal

A pilot usually measures what its sponsor can see: the user's time, the team's output, or the budget. It is harder to see a nonuser's lost choice. And asking AI to role-play the missing person does not turn that simulation into their testimony: one preprint found that a ten-persona panel predicted real headline clicks *worse* than a plain model baseline on the distinguishable tests it examined. That result is about aggregate headline clicks, not all audience research. ^[raw/articles/synthetic-personas-real-audience-2609.25010.md]

## Why It Matters for Moving Beyond Prompting

The operator asks whether the AI produced the requested result. The orchestrator also asks **who bears the cost if that result is used**. A successful pilot for its users can still fail someone who never opted in. That is a design question, not a request for everyone to be comfortable with AI.

## The Bridge

Before scaling, make an **absent-person row** in the workflow brief:

| Ask | Write down |
|---|---|
| Who is affected but not using this? | A specific role: customer, student, family, coworker, bystander |
| What could change for them? | Privacy, access, time, work, reputation, opportunity |
| How will we learn whether it helped or harmed? | A real measure, appropriate feedback, or a small comparison with the current process—not a simulated person's approval |
| What can they do about it? | Notice, consent where appropriate, a usable alternative, a named human, and a route to challenge |

In schools, use **hypothetical scenarios or approved, non-identifiable examples** for this exercise. Do not put student records into an unapproved tool or treat AI-generated student or parent personas as real community input. Pair this planning check with [[Procedural Standing]]: this page asks who is missing **before** rollout; that page supplies the appeal route **after** something goes wrong.

## Try This

**Five-minute absent-person check:** Choose one proposed AI use. Write the name of one affected *role* not in the planning meeting, one possible benefit, one possible cost, and one real way to hear from or protect that group. If your only evidence is an AI-generated persona, mark the benefit **unverified** and keep the pilot small.
