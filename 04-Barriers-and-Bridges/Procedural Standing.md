# Procedural Standing

## What It Is

Procedural standing is the feeling that you are not trapped under an AI system. You have a handle: a way to ask why, challenge a result, appeal a decision, opt out, or route the issue to a human who actually owns the call.

This matters because people do not judge AI only by whether it is accurate. They also ask: **Can I contest it? Can I understand what happened? Can I get unstuck? Does anyone answer for this?** When the answer is no, the system may work technically and still feel illegitimate.

## Why It's Normal

If an AI writes a bad paragraph, you can delete it. If an AI refuses to answer, ranks your application, persuades you in a conversation, or shapes a decision you cannot appeal, the stakes change. You are no longer just using a tool. You are inside a procedure — or inside the absence of one.

That is why this barrier shows up as resistance. People are not always afraid of AI capability. Often, they are reacting to **power without a handle**.

Recent sources point in the same direction:

- Users prefer a hallucinated answer over a dead-end refusal, even when they know hallucinations are less accurate. A refusal that strands people creates its own risk.
- A short warning that AI can be prompted to persuade cut political belief change by about half. The warning worked because it named the mechanism before the interaction.
- Job seekers value appeal, opt-out, independent audit, and meaningful human involvement even beyond statistical error correction. The right to invoke a process has dignity value.
- Governance attention does not always match sector vulnerability. A thick policy stack can still miss the people and risks most exposed.

## Why It Matters for Moving Beyond Prompting

The operator asks, accepts, edits, and moves on. The orchestrator designs the conditions under which AI work can be trusted, challenged, corrected, or stopped.

Procedural standing is the bridge between those worlds. Without it, delegation feels like surrender. With it, delegation becomes a managed relationship: the AI can act, but people keep standing.

## The Bridge

Use the **Contestability Check** before giving an AI workflow more autonomy.

| Question | What It Protects | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Can the user tell what mode the AI is in?** | Refusing, persuading, ranking, recommending, drafting, or deciding should not blur together | “This system is recommending, not deciding.” |
| **Can the user ask why?** | A useful explanation tied to evidence, policy, or missing information | “I can’t answer because X is missing; here is what I can safely provide.” |
| **Can the user contest or route?** | Appeal, opt out, ask a human, or trigger review when consequences matter | “Click here for human review before this rejection is finalized.” |
| **Can someone verify the procedure happened?** | Logs, receipts, timestamps, audit trails, and owner names | “Appeal received by [owner] at [time]; decision due by [date].” |

The small move: before scaling an AI workflow, add one visible handle. Not ten. One. A refusal next step. An appeal route. A human owner. A receipt. A disclosure sentence that names persuasive intent.

## Try This

**5-Minute Exercise: Add One Handle**

Pick one AI-assisted workflow you use or oversee.

1. Write the sentence: “If this goes wrong, the person affected can ______.”
2. If you cannot finish the sentence, you found the barrier.
3. Add one handle:
   - a refusal-next-step line,
   - a human review route,
   - an appeal option,
   - a visible audit log,
   - or a pre-use warning that names the AI’s possible agenda.
4. Run the workflow again and ask: “Does the person now have standing, or just a nicer message?”
