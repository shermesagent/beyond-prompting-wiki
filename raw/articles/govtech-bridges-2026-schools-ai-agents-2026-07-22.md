# Bridges 2026: How Schools Are Putting AI Agents to Work

**Source:** GovTech / Center for Digital Education, Julia Gilban-Cohen, 2026-07-22 (Bridges conference, New York City)

**Summary:** Education leaders shared production examples of AI agents — not chatbots — running in day-to-day operations: tutoring students, processing enrollment workflows, and supporting staff with administrative tasks. The biggest divide between institutions was not whether agents have value, but **how much autonomy and data access they are willing to grant**.

- **Agent vs. chatbot definition** (Joe Siedlecki, Amira Learning): a chatbot responds to prompts; a real agent makes decisions in service of a specific goal, observes outcomes, and changes its action based on results. "It's making decisions pinned back to a specific goal, and the intent is to observe and change action."
- **Milwaukee Area Technical College** (Sadique Isahaku): deliberately cautious. Uses faculty-controlled agents from Gemini Notebook for tutoring outside office hours; won't give agents broad access to institutional data ("we don't know yet... collect data that probably shouldn't go out to the public"). Automates a monthly enrollment reporting process that determines institutional funding — but **keeps the very last verification step for a human**: "When we're talking $150 million a year... you do want somebody just at the very end to verify those before we file them with the state."
- **Johnston Community College** (Carrie Pitts-Densmore): the opposite posture — agents deeply integrated into enrollment operations to scale amid growth and staffing constraints: "We couldn't hire as quickly as we were growing... we assign them jobs, and the goal is to move the student through the enrollment cycle from inquiry to completion." She had 12 agents performing tasks on her behalf while at the conference.
- **Peninsula School District, WA** (Kris Hagel, CIO): piloted AI agents last year; plans to make them available to every adult staffer this fall — "we're working on building out the skills that we assign to the agents that users can use." Also: "Agents is one of the most abused terms in ed tech right now."
- **Culture over deployment:** Pitts-Densmore: "It's not 'set-it-and-forget-it.'" Identify internal champions, build a culture where you can "fail forward" without being penalized. Isahaku: faculty conversations — instructors showing successful classroom uses — drive adoption more than top-down directives.
- Hagel: "I think every K-12 public school district right now is running low on money" — agents are a capacity lever when neither systems nor people can be added.

**Why it matters for the shift:** The operator→orchestrator divide shows up inside real institutions as the *autonomy divide*: identical technology, radically different decisions about how much the agent may do on its own. The working deployments all kept a human judgment point at the end (verification before state filing) and treated agents as skill-assigned workers, not open-ended chatbots. Production agents are adopted through champions and fail-forward culture — not memos.
