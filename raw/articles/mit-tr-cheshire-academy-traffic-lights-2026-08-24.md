# How to encourage smarter AI use in the classroom

MIT Technology Review, 2026-08-24, by Peter Hall (Making AI Work newsletter)

**Summary (full text read):**

Case study of **Cheshire Academy**, a private boarding and day school in Connecticut with ~400 students in grades 9–12. Key facts:

- **Staff approach:** Administrators don't force instructors to use AI, though the "vast majority" use it in some way (ChatGPT, Perplexity, plus specialized tools like MagicSchool). On consultants' advice, the school trained staff on *general techniques* for using AI — including how to craft prompts AND the technology's limits (incorrect and biased responses) — instead of prescribing specific tech.
- **Teachers use AI to prepare class materials**: lesson planning, grading rubrics. Some want AI feedback for students, but concerns over quality, personalization, and privacy have blocked that so far.
- **Miriam Przybyla-Baum (French teacher, ~30 years of materials):** doesn't use AI herself but teaches with it. Assignment 1: students let an LLM edit their homework, then go through the edits and decide which were correct and which removed their voice. Assignment 2: students anonymously grade each other's AI-assisted assignments, annotating which parts they think are AI-assisted.
- **Traffic-light assignments:** school-wide, assignments are now labelled like traffic lights — green = AI fully allowed, yellow = some tools allowed while others banned (e.g., spell-check allowed, chatbot not), red = no AI.
- **Student AI Council:** a pilot program in which students create media and lead discussions about healthy AI use — pushing students to reflect on how AI should and shouldn't be used to benefit their community.
- **The tool (MagicSchool):** generates questions, assignments, quizzes, worksheets across subjects/grade levels; grading rubric generator; presentations, lesson plans, administrative reports. ~$100/year for individual unlimited plan. General chatbots used for administrative tasks.
- **"How to apply this" (from the article):**
  1. *Meet students where they're at* — students will be tempted to try AI; counter misinformation and teach healthy strategies and relationships.
  2. *Model best practices* — AI is very good at automating tasks but struggles with precision and voice; students who see authority figures using AI lazily may internalize cutting corners.
  3. *Refine and replace* — AI is great for brainstorming lesson plans and extra problem sets, "especially for teachers early in their careers who don't have a big problem bank already built up"; but check every citation, equation, and statement — using it for final drafts can result in assignments that confuse students.

**Why it matters for the wiki:** a real school's governance answer to the adoption question — explicit interaction structure (traffic lights), reflection exercises (LLM-edit judging), and student participation (AI Council). The LLM-edit assignment is literally From Author to Editor as pedagogy.
