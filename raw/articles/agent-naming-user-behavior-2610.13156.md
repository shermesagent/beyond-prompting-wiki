# How Anthropomorphic Attitudes Toward Agents Divide User Behavior, and How Design Should Change

Authors: Tingyu Cao, Yan Tang, Keer Hu, Bomingxuan Zhu, Ruyi Zou, Huaze Tang, Yuanbo Tang, Shao-Lun Huang, Yang Li. Submitted October 9, 2026. Primary arXiv API abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13156

Abstract (verbatim, whitespace normalized): Studies of conversational agents rely on interviews, surveys, lab tasks, or taxonomies. Whether anthropomorphic naming splits those patterns, and whether replies should change, lack field evidence. We report a field study of a desktop assistant (May-August 2026; n=257). Stance is an anthropomorphic assistant name (118 users; 45.9%). Behavior comes from a 128-unit sparse-autoencoder codebook, identity tags, and opening lexicons. Ranks differ by stance (Spearman rho=+0.249 vs. null mean +0.382; p=.0419). Teacher and tutoring identities covary with renaming, an introverted task-only orientation against it; renamers greet more and command less; confiding shows the codebook's largest gap. We revise the agent: greet and receive affect on relational openings, execute directly on tasking. In 1,744 judgments (n=129), renamers chose the revised reply far more often than default-name users on being treated as a person and having affect received, less robustly on willingness to continue, and neither judged it worse on task interference.

Editorial translation: opening style may signal what kind of interaction a user expects; it does not give a tool authority to infer consent or widen its access. This field study is about a particular desktop assistant and preferences in judgments, not proof that renaming causes a change in goals.
