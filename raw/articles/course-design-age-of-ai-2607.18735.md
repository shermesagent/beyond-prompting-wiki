# Course Design in the Age of AI

**Source:** arXiv:2607.18735 (Davies), v3 dated 2026-09-02 (originally July 2026)

**Summary:** An economics model of learning-by-doing and course design. A **myopic student** faces a sequence of tasks he can either work on or delegate to AI. Work requires costly effort but builds skill; delegation requires no effort but builds no skill. A teacher designs the task sequence (the "course") to maximize the student's skill development, given the student's freedom to choose work or delegation.

Three results:

1. **Without AI**, the teacher makes earlier tasks more effort-intensive (to build foundational skill) and later tasks more skill-intensive.
2. **With AI**, the teacher must redesign early tasks to induce effort — and even so, skill development is lower than in the no-AI world. The tool that lets students skip effort changes what a course must be.
3. **If AI complements effort** (helps you work rather than replacing working), then improvements in AI quality make **high-skill students learn faster while low-skill students learn slower**. Better AI widens the gap unless the course actively compensates.

The practical read for the shift: delegation is a loan against your own skill. When the machine gets better, the people who keep doing the effortful version of the work pull further ahead — and the people who delegate the effortful parts fall further behind. Course (or workflow) design — deliberately keeping some tasks effortful — is the compensating lever.

**Bottom line:** a better model does not automatically make you better. It makes the *skilled who keep working* better faster, and it makes the *unskilled who delegate* worse by comparison. What you choose to keep effortful is the difference.
