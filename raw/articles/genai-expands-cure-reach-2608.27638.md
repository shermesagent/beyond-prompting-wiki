# Generative AI Expands the Intellectual Reach of Course-Based Undergraduate Research Experiences (CUREs)

**Source:** Aditi Babar, Kristin J. Davin & Alex Dornburg, arXiv:2608.27638 (cs.CY), published 2026-08-27

**Summary:** Course-based undergraduate research experiences (CUREs) broaden access to authentic scientific inquiry through responsive instructor support as research problems become increasingly complex. Generative AI may extend this support by providing individualized assistance that adapts as student needs change. Using longitudinal qualitative data collected across three semesters of a bioinformatics and genomics CURE, the paper shows that GenAI expanded the intellectual reach of the research experience in three distinct ways. First, personalized, on-demand scaffolding allowed students to move beyond the boundaries of instructor expertise and transform their own interests into researchable inquiry — all teams developed distinct self-directed projects rather than selecting instructor-provided topics. Second, GenAI became part of the distributed cognitive system of research teams, helping novice researchers communicate and coordinate across differentiated expertise without eliminating specialization. Third, expanded capability did not replace the need for disciplinary judgment: students increasingly validated, revised, or rejected AI-generated contributions, such that research independence emerged through retained intellectual responsibility. Together, these findings suggest GenAI can extend the reach of research education by expanding what novice researchers can investigate, how they can collaborate, and the level of responsibility they can assume while preserving human judgment central to authentic scientific inquiry.

**Key takeaways for the wiki:**
- AI that expands reach does not have to replace judgment: in this study, students used the AI to go further, then validated, revised, and rejected its contributions — independence came FROM the retained responsibility, not despite the AI.
- The positive arc of the shift: AI raises the floor (access, scaffolding, coordination) while human judgment raises the ceiling (deciding what's good, what's wrong, what to keep).
- Scaffolding that adapts to the learner turns instructor-expertise limits into researchable questions — the same "personalized, on-demand support" pattern that powers good orchestration in any domain.
- Watch the mechanism: teams kept specialization (each member's distinct expertise) while AI handled coordination — AI as connective tissue, not replacement.
- For educators and leaders: the design target is "students increasingly validate, revise, or reject AI output" — that's the review-first pattern becoming a habit, and it's the practical definition of moving beyond prompting in a learning environment.
